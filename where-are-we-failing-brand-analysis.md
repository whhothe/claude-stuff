# WHERE ARE WE FAILING? — Brand-Wide Analysis

## The One-Line Answer

**Abyssal is not failing because the format stopped working. Abyssal is failing because it scaled the format like a factory — more volume, faster output, identical packaging — in a medium that rewards the opposite: scarcity, variation, and surprise.**

---

## The Proof That This Isn't a Format Problem

Before diagnosing the failure, it's critical to establish what IS and ISN'T broken:

**The niche is thriving.** Channels in the same space are exploding:
- A channel with **1,550 subscribers** (Absolute Chaos Moments) got 1.19M views on a single video
- **SIRIUS** (300K subs, 18 videos total) averages **2.81M views per video** — 11.5x higher than AD1
- **Chilling Scares** (2.86M subs) maintains 318K Day-1 median views with steady +18.6% annual sub growth
- **BeyondTheBlue** launched Dec 2024, hit 217K subs and $29.8K/month revenue in under 2 years
- **Chezzoid** launched Aug 2025, hit 145K subs and $19.3K/month in 13 months

**The audience still likes the content.** Like-to-view ratios across all three channels have remained stable or improved:
- AD1: 1.86% (2024) → 2.91% (Jun 2026) — actually went UP as views collapsed
- AD2: 1.8-3.0% — stable throughout
- AO: 2.1% (2025) → 2.3% (2026) — stable

**This means the people who FIND the videos still enjoy them at the same rate they always have.** The problem is reach — YouTube is showing the content to fewer people. The question is: why?

## WHAT IS FATIGUING ABOUT ABYSSAL ACROSS ALL THREE CHANNELS

### 1. Title Monoculture: One Formula × 328 Videos

This is the single most damaging pattern across the entire brand.

| Channel | Videos | "Iceberg" in Title | "Disturbing" in Title |
|---|---|---|---|
| AD1 | 136 | 131 (96%) | 131 (96%) |
| AD2 | 80 | 79 (99%) | 79 (99%) |
| AO | 112 | 111 (99%) | 109 (97%) |
| **Network Total** | **328** | **321 (98%)** | **319 (97%)** |

**321 out of 328 videos across the entire Abyssal Network are titled "the Most Disturbing [X] Iceberg."** This is not a brand identity — this is a brand prison.

What this does to the algorithm:
- YouTube uses title, thumbnail, and metadata signals to decide WHO to recommend a video to. When 321 videos share the same title structure, YouTube cannot differentiate them. It doesn't know if "Most Disturbing Dashcam Iceberg" should reach a different audience than "Most Disturbing CCTV Iceberg" — so it defaults to showing both to the same shrinking pool of people who clicked the last "Most Disturbing [X] Iceberg."
- CTR fatigue compounds. A subscriber who sees "Most Disturbing [X] Iceberg" in their feed for the 50th time stops clicking — not because the content is bad, but because the TITLE looks identical to the last 49 videos they were shown.
- New viewers are excluded. "Iceberg" is niche format jargon. The word signals "this is a community format you need to already understand." Every top-performing competitor avoids it: "120 Incredible Moments Caught on Camera" (6.78M), "When Truckers Put An End To Road Madness" (2.57M), "Icy Road Madness Caught on Camera" (1.70M). These titles are instantly parseable by someone who has never heard of icebergs.

**What competitors do instead:**
- Chilling Scares uses 4-5 different formulas: "[Number] Most Disturbing [Topic]," "Explained," "Turned Out to Be True," "Solved," unique framings like "28 Minutes of Purely Horrific Information"
- GOHA Moments, DICA, MachRush, SNAP MOMENTS — the fastest-growing channels in this niche — each use 6+ title variations: numbered compilations, temporal hooks, specific scenarios, emotional triggers, "Best of Month"

**One non-iceberg title on AD1 outperformed the 2026 average.** "4chan Threads That Triggered FBI Investigations" pulled 407K views — above the channel's 252K average for 2026 — proving a different title CAN reach a different audience even on a channel the algorithm has learned to suppress.

**Abyssal asks its audience for 17x more watch time than its closest competitor.** No audience can sustain that. The inevitable result: most videos get skipped → YouTube sees declining CTR and engagement → algorithm reduces impressions → views drop → the response has been to upload MORE to compensate → which worsens the cycle.

YouTube typically gives a video 5-7 days to find its audience through suggested and browse. At 3.9-day gaps, every video cuts the previous one's testing window in half. Multiply this by three channels running the same cycle and the network is eating itself.

## WHAT I WOULD CHANGE TO HELP THE BASELINE GO UP

### Change 1: Title Diversification (Zero production cost, immediate impact)

**Current state:** 321/328 videos are "Most Disturbing [X] Iceberg"
**Target state:** Iceberg stays as ONE of 4-5 title frameworks in rotation

Proposed rotation:
1. **KEEP "Iceberg"** for 1-2 genuinely deep, high-demand topics per month per channel. The iceberg format is the brand's identity — it doesn't disappear, it becomes the premium offering instead of the default.
2. **ADD numbered compilations:** "[Number] Most [Adjective] [Specific Topic] Ever Caught on Camera." This formula generated the biggest outlier videos across the entire niche — GOHA's "120 Incredible Moments" (6.78M), MTH's "390 Incredible Moments" (4.71M), DICA's "80 Shocking Plane Crashes" (2.04M).
3. **ADD temporal hooks:** "Best of [Month/Year]" or "[Topic] — 2026 Update." This lets the same channel revisit the same topic monthly without seeming repetitive: "Best Dashcam Moments — September 2026" feels completely different from "Dashcam Footage Vol. 6."
4. **ADD specific scenario titles:** "When [Specific Situation] Goes Wrong" or "The [Specific Event] That [Consequence]." MachRush's "When Truckers Put An End To Road Madness" (2.57M) proves this angle works at scale.
5. **ADD resolution/revelation framing:** "The [X] That Turned Out to Be Real" or "[X] Explained — What Actually Happened." Chilling Scares' "Conspiracy Theories That Turned Out to Be True" (7.2M) and "FBI's 10 Most Wanted Explained" (9.9M) are the proof.

**Why this works:** Each framework signals a DIFFERENT value proposition to a DIFFERENT audience segment. A viewer who's tired of icebergs might click "When Elevator Rides Turn Into Nightmares." A viewer who never understood what an iceberg was might click "120 Most Terrifying Moments Caught on Camera." The content can be identical — the title determines who YouTube shows it to.

**Data supporting this:** AD1's one non-iceberg title — "4chan Threads That Triggered FBI Investigations" (407K) — outperformed the 2026 channel average (252K) by 61%. One title change. Same content quality. Better reach.

### Change 2: Upload Cadence Reduction (Saves resources, improves per-video performance)

**Current state:** ~20 videos/month network-wide (6-8 per channel)
**Target state:** ~12 videos/month network-wide (4 per channel)

This is the single highest-leverage structural change because it addresses multiple problems simultaneously:

| Benefit | Data Evidence |
|---|---|
| Each video gets longer algorithmic runway | AD1's Sep 2025 (4 videos, 602K avg) vs Jun 2026 (8 videos, 142K avg) — 4.2x difference |
| Reduces viewer time ask from 18.3 hrs to ~7.5 hrs/month | Still higher than Chilling Scares (1.1 hrs) but within sustainable range |
| Reduces network-wide feed flooding | YouTube won't show 3 identical-looking videos from the same brand in one browse session |
| More production time per video = better content | SIRIUS: 18 videos, 2.81M avg. AD1: 136 videos, 602K avg (declining to 138K). Quality → selectivity → performance |
| Slows topic exhaustion | At 4/month per channel, A-tier topics last 3x longer before barrel-scraping |

**The competitive evidence is overwhelming:**

| Upload Pattern | Channels | Avg Views/Video |
|---|---|---|
| ≤4/month (selective) | SIRIUS, Chilling Scares, Nexpo, Storified | 1.36M avg |
| 5-8/month (volume) | AD1, AD2, AO | 337K avg (and declining) |

The selective group outperforms by 4x on average.

**The revenue objection:** "But fewer videos means fewer monetized views." Short-term, yes. But AD1 currently generates ~1M views/month across 7-8 videos (128K avg each). If cadence dropped to 4 videos/month and per-video performance recovered to even 350K avg (where AD1 was in Q4 2025 with fewer uploads), total monthly views would be 1.4M — a 40% INCREASE in total views with half the production effort. The math works because fewer, better-performing videos generate more total views than many suppressed ones.

### Change 3: Kill Visible Sequel Numbering

**Current state:** "Vol. 2," "Vol. 3," through "Vol. 7" across all channels
**Target state:** Every video has a standalone title that doesn't require prior viewing

This doesn't mean stop covering the same topics. It means stop LABELING them as sequels.

**Instead of:** "Most Disturbing Dashcam Footage Iceberg Vol. 6"
**Use:** "The Dashcam Footage From Russia That Went Viral — Black Ice, Road Rage, and Unexplainable Encounters"

Both videos can contain the same content. But the second title:
- Doesn't tell a new viewer "you missed 5 episodes"
- Has specific visual keywords (Russia, black ice, road rage) that YouTube can match to specific audiences
- Creates its own search footprint separate from Vol 1-5
- Doesn't trigger "I already saw this series" CTR fatigue in existing subscribers

**Sequel decay data proves the urgency:**
- AD1 Dashcam: Vol 1 (1.19M) → Vol 6 (61K) = -95%
- AD1 CCTV: Vol 1 (3.17M) → Vol 4 (132K) = -96%
- AO Talent Show: Vol 1 (4.67M) → Vol 3 (77K) = -83%
- AD2 Theme Park: Vol 3 (1.19M) → Vol 5 (91K) = -92%

**The one exception that proves the rule:** AD2's "Things on Asian Internet" went FROM Vol 1 (603K) TO Vol 2 (1.17M) — a +94% increase. Why? Because the topic was genuinely novel and the title was specific enough to attract new viewers who hadn't seen Vol 1. The numbering didn't hurt because the audience was still growing for that topic. For every other series where the Vol 1 audience has already been captured, the numbering is pure downside.

### Change 4: Duration Optimization (Gradual, not drastic)

**Current state:** Network average 55 minutes (AD1: 59, AO: 57, AD2: 48)
**Target state:** 30-40 minutes for standard uploads; 55+ only for premium event icebergs

The competitive data on duration is clear:

| Duration | Avg Views (competitor outliers 1M+) |
|---|---|
| Under 20 min | Works for hyper-specific topics (cruise ships, black ice) |
| **20-35 min** | **Sweet spot: 28 min average for 1M+ outlier videos** |
| 35-50 min | Viable for mega-compilations (300+ clips) |
| 50-60 min | AD's current default — works only for genuinely deep content |
| 60+ min | Works only for event-level content (yearly compilations, MTH's "390 clips") |

**Why shorter works better for algorithm reach:**
A viewer who watches 60% of a 35-minute video (21 minutes of watch time) sends a better algorithmic signal than a viewer who watches 35% of a 60-minute video (21 minutes of watch time). Same absolute watch time — but the first case shows 60% retention vs 35% retention. YouTube promotes the higher-retention video.

**How to implement without losing depth:**
- Cut iceberg levels from 7 to 4-5 per video. The remaining levels become a separate video with a DIFFERENT title.
- This actually creates more content opportunities: one 7-level, 60-minute iceberg becomes two 4-level, 30-minute videos — each with its own title, its own thumbnail, its own audience targeting.
- Reserve 55+ minute icebergs for quarterly "event" uploads on the biggest topics. Make length itself a signal of importance.

### Change 5: Channel Lane Reinforcement (Already started, needs sharpening)

AO already has the right idea — TV/entertainment content that's distinct from AD1's footage compilations. This is the model for the network, and the data proves it:

| Channel | Content Lane | RPM | Monthly Revenue |
|---|---|---|---|
| AD1 | Real-world footage / caught on camera | — | ~$8-10K est. |
| AD2 | AD1 sequel overflow (no unique lane) | $12 base | $12,804 |
| AO | TV shows / entertainment | $7.72 total | $26,674 |

**AO earns the most revenue because it has the clearest content lane.** TV/entertainment content attracts entertainment advertisers (streaming services, movie studios) with higher CPMs. AD2 has the highest base RPM ($12) but the lowest reach because it has no unique identity.

Each channel needs to OWN a lane so clearly that the algorithm understands who to recommend it to:

| Channel | Current Lane | Sharpened Lane |
|---|---|---|
| AD1 | Generic "disturbing" footage | **Real-world caught-on-camera**: Dashcam, CCTV, doorbell, drone, bodycam — anything filmed by a real device in the real world |
| AD2 | Vol 3+ sequel dump | **Regional/cultural deep dives**: Asian Internet (its one breakout), country-specific content, cultural phenomena — the "What's on [Region]'s Internet" brand |
| AO | TV/movies/entertainment | **Named TV shows & on-screen incidents**: Specific show names (TLC, Survivor, AGT), on-camera accidents, behind-the-scenes revelations |

**AD2's lane shift is the most important.** The channel's own data proves "Asian Internet" is the only content that GREW between volumes (+94%). Everything else on AD2 is a dying sequel of an AD1 series. If AD2 pivots to own the "regional internet" space, it finally has a discovery pathway that doesn't depend on AD1's audience.

## WHAT I THINK YOU ARE PROTECTING

This is the hardest part of the question because it requires naming the sacred cows — the things the brand holds onto not because the data supports them, but because they FEEL essential to the identity.

### 1. You're Protecting Revenue-Through-Volume

**The belief:** More videos = more monetized views = more revenue. Reducing uploads from 20/month to 12/month means fewer videos earning money.

**Why it's wrong:** The math has flipped. In Q1 2025, 4 videos/month on AD1 generated ~6M total monthly views (1.5M × 4). Today, 8 videos/month generate ~1M total monthly views (128K × 8). **Doubling output cut total monthly views by 83%.** The volume strategy is no longer producing more revenue — it's producing less revenue at higher production cost. Every additional video uploaded cannibalizes the previous one's impression cycle, resulting in LESS total reach than fewer, stronger uploads would generate.

The fear is that cutting to 4/month means losing 50% of videos. The reality is that 4 videos at 350K avg (AD1's Q4 2025 performance with lower cadence) = 1.4M monthly views, versus 8 videos at 128K avg = 1.02M monthly views. **Fewer videos would likely generate MORE total views AND revenue.**

### 2. You're Protecting the Iceberg as Identity

**The belief:** The iceberg format IS Abyssal. If we stop making icebergs, we stop being Abyssal. Every video must be an iceberg or the brand loses its identity.

**Why it's partially wrong:** The iceberg format is genuinely Abyssal's competitive advantage — no competitor does the descent structure as well. But making EVERY video an iceberg turns an advantage into a constraint. The format should be the brand's signature offering — the event, the premium product — not the default for every upload. Chilling Scares' identity is "disturbing content" — not "lists." They use the list format most of the time, but when they do "28 Minutes of Purely Horrific Information" (2.2M) or "The FBI's 10 Most Wanted Explained" (9.9M), the format break refreshes the audience's attention and the algorithm's perception of the channel.

**What Abyssal should protect:** The iceberg format as the CROWN JEWEL — 1-2 per month per channel, on the biggest topics, at full depth. What should NOT be protected: the requirement that every video be an iceberg. A numbered compilation, a "Best of Month," a single-story deep dive — these can all coexist on the same channel without diluting the brand. They actually STRENGTHEN the iceberg brand by making each iceberg feel like an event rather than the default.

### 3. You're Protecting Production Ease

**The belief:** The current system is production-efficient. Same template, same title formula, same thumbnail style, same duration, same structure → the team can produce videos fast with minimal creative decision-making.

**Why it's a trap:** Templated production IS efficient — for production. But it's anti-efficient for growth. The production template has eliminated the creative friction that forces new ideas. Every video follows the same rails: pick a topic, research 7 levels, record narration, edit footage into iceberg descent, use the same title format. This is a factory line, and factory lines produce commodity products. The algorithm can tell.

**What's being traded:** Production speed for creative stagnation. The channels haven't had a genuinely new FORMAT idea since launch. Not a new topic (those still come) — a new WAY of presenting content. The last format innovation on AD1 was the iceberg itself, in October 2024.

### 4. You're Protecting the Multi-Channel Revenue Multiplier

**The belief:** Three channels = three revenue streams. Three AdSense accounts. Three subscriber bases. Three chances to appear in a viewer's feed.

**Why it's hurting more than helping:** The multi-channel structure multiplies production but divides audience. A viewer who subscribes to AD1 is likely being recommended AD2 and AO content, which means:
- YouTube counts impressions across all three channels toward that viewer's "saturation" with Abyssal content
- The viewer sees 20 identical-looking thumbnails per month instead of 7 — accelerating CTR fatigue
- Each channel's subscriber base is cannibalized by the others' recommendations
- AD2 specifically has no standalone reason to exist — its own description says it's a "Vol 3+ dump"

**This doesn't mean the channels should merge.** But it means the current setup — where all three produce identical-looking content under the same title formula — doesn't multiply reach, it fragments it. The fix is lane specialization (already discussed above), not structural changes. Each channel needs to look, feel, and title content differently enough that YouTube treats them as genuinely different channels, not three avatars of the same brand.

### 5. You're Protecting Long Duration as a Revenue Strategy

**The belief:** Longer videos = more mid-roll ad placements = higher revenue per video. A 60-minute video can have 6-8 mid-rolls. A 30-minute video can have 3-4. So longer = better for monetization.

**Why it's self-defeating at current scale:** The mid-roll advantage only works if viewers WATCH through the mid-rolls. AD1's 59-minute videos at 128K avg views likely have 30-40% average retention (typical for long-form compilations) — meaning most viewers drop off before the 3rd mid-roll. A 35-minute video with 60% retention would serve mid-rolls to MORE viewers because a higher percentage of the audience reaches each one.

More importantly: the network's RPM is already strong. AD2 has the highest base RPM ($12) in the entire niche dataset. AO earns $7.72 total RPM. The monetization per view is excellent. The problem is not how much each view earns — it's how many views each video gets. Optimizing for view count (through shorter, more discoverable videos) will generate more total revenue than optimizing for revenue-per-view (through longer videos that fewer people watch).

### 6. You're Protecting the Sequel Pipeline

**The belief:** Popular series deserve continuations. If Dashcam Vol 1 got 1.19M views, there's an audience waiting for Vol 2, Vol 3, Vol 4. Sequels are also production-efficient — the research pipeline is already built, the format is proven, the footage sources are known.

**Why it's destroying reach:** The data is unequivocal. Across 54 sequel videos network-wide, the average decay from best volume to latest volume is -75%. Every Vol 4+ video is performing at less than a quarter of the series' peak. Yet the network continues producing them because:
- They're easy to produce (the research pipeline exists)
- They fill the upload schedule (needed at 20/month)
- They feel "safe" (the topic already worked once)

But sequels are not safe. They're the HIGHEST-RISK content the network produces because they carry built-in CTR fatigue ("I already saw this series") AND built-in algorithmic suppression ("viewers who watched earlier volumes showed declining engagement"). An original video on a fresh topic has NO negative signal history — it starts from zero. A Vol 5 sequel starts with YouTube already knowing that each successive volume performed worse.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

**Abyssal's format is not aging. The format is being overproduced.** The iceberg is still a genuinely superior structure — no competitor in this niche builds content with the same depth, escalation, or viewer investment. That's a real competitive advantage. The problem is that the network treated its competitive advantage like a commodity and mass-produced it until the market couldn't absorb the volume.

The fix is not to abandon the iceberg. The fix is to make the iceberg rare again — the event people show up for — and surround it with complementary formats that keep the channels active and discoverable without exhausting the audience. The brand is protecting its assembly line when it should be protecting its scarcity.

**What needs to happen for the baseline to go up:**
1. Fewer videos per month (each one gets a longer algorithmic runway + less self-cannibalization)
2. More title variation (each video can reach a DIFFERENT audience segment)
3. Shorter standard duration (higher retention % = stronger algorithm signal)
4. Standalone titles (every video is discoverable without needing prior viewing)
5. Sharper channel lanes (the algorithm sees three distinct products instead of one fragmented brand)

None of these changes require abandoning the iceberg format, merging channels, or rebranding. They are all tactical adjustments to packaging, pacing, and positioning — working WITH the existing structure to reverse the decline that overproduction created.
