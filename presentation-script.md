## INTRO (1-2 minutes)

Hi, my name is [YOUR NAME], and this is my application for the Creative Strategist position at NIA Production for the Abyssal Network.

I was asked two questions. First: where is the Abyssal Network failing — brand-wide and per channel — and why are baseline views declining? Second: pitch three new video ideas, one per channel, with the framework, the hook, what I'd change, how my version differs, and why it beats the original.

I spent considerable time researching the network — all 330 videos across all three channels, the competitive landscape, the analytics, the content patterns. What I'm going to present isn't guesswork. Every number I reference comes from the channels' own public data and YouTube analytics. Every case in my video pitches is verified and sourced. And every recommendation I make is a tactical adjustment within the existing channel structure — I'm not here to propose tearing anything down.

Let's start with the diagnosis.

## QUESTION 1: WHERE IS THE ABYSSAL NETWORK FAILING? (10-12 minutes)

### The Good News First (1 minute)

Before I say what's wrong, I want to be clear about what's NOT wrong.

The niche is thriving. Channels in the exact same space are exploding right now. A channel called SIRIUS with only 300K subs and just 18 total videos is averaging **2.81 million views per video**. A channel called Absolute Chaos Moments with **1,550 subscribers** got 1.19 million views on a single video. Chilling Scares at 2.86 million subs is maintaining 318K Day-1 median views with steady 18.6% annual subscriber growth. New channels like BeyondTheBlue and Chezzoid have launched in the last two years and are already earning $20-30K per month.

The audience still likes Abyssal's content. The like-to-view ratio across all three channels has stayed stable or actually gone UP — AD1 went from 1.86% in 2024 to 2.91% in mid-2026. The people who find the videos still enjoy them at the same rate they always have.

So the format isn't broken. The niche isn't dying. The audience isn't disengaged. The problem is reach — YouTube is showing the content to fewer people. The question is: why?

### The Six Systemic Failures (7-8 minutes)

I identified six systemic failures that are shared across all three channels. These aren't channel-specific problems — they're network-wide patterns that explain why three channels with very different subscriber counts have all converged to nearly identical performance.

**Failure Number One: Title Monoculture.**

This is the single most damaging pattern. Out of 328 videos across the entire Abyssal Network, **321 are titled "the Most Disturbing [something] Iceberg."** That's 98%. Ninety-eight percent of every video the network has ever produced follows one title formula.

What this does to the algorithm: YouTube uses title signals to decide who to recommend a video to. When 321 videos share the same structure, YouTube cannot differentiate them. It doesn't know if "Most Disturbing Dashcam Iceberg" should reach a different audience than "Most Disturbing CCTV Iceberg" — so it defaults to showing both to the same shrinking pool of people who clicked the last one. The CTR fatigue compounds — a subscriber who sees "Most Disturbing [X] Iceberg" for the 50th time stops clicking. Not because the content is bad, but because the title looks identical to the last 49.

Compare this to what competitors do. Chilling Scares uses 4-5 different title frameworks. The fastest-growing channels in this niche — GOHA, DICA, MachRush, SNAP MOMENTS — each use 6 or more title variations. Their outlier videos use titles like "120 Incredible Moments Caught on Camera" at 6.78 million views, or "When Truckers Put An End To Road Madness" at 2.57 million views. These titles are instantly parseable by someone who has never heard of an iceberg format.

And here's the proof that title variation works even on Abyssal: AD1's one non-iceberg title — "4chan Threads That Triggered FBI Investigations" — pulled 407K views, which is above the 2026 channel average. One title change. Same content quality. Better reach.

**Failure Number Two: The Volume Death Spiral.**

The network's combined output is approximately **20 videos per month** — that's 7-8 from AD1, 6-7 from AD2, and 6 from AO. At an average duration of 55 minutes, that's **18.3 hours of content every single month** from the Abyssal brand.

For comparison: Chilling Scares asks about 1.1 hours per month. SIRIUS asks 0.3 hours per month. Abyssal asks its audience for **17 times more watch time** than its closest competitor.

The data across all three channels shows the same inverse correlation. Every time uploads increased, per-video views decreased.

On AD1: when they uploaded 4 videos per month in late 2024, average views were over 1 million. When uploads increased to 7-8 per month in 2026, average views crashed to 140K. That's a **-87% decline** while output doubled.

AD2 shows the same pattern: 3 uploads per month gave them 601K average. 8 uploads per month gave them 84K average.

AO: 3 per month, 585K average. 7 per month, 127K average.

The mechanism is self-cannibalization. When a new video uploads every 3.9 days, each video cuts the previous one's algorithmic testing window in half. YouTube typically gives a video 5-7 days to find its audience. At 3.9-day gaps, that window gets shortened before the video has had a chance to reach its full potential.

**Failure Number Three: Channel Convergence.**

This is the most damning piece of evidence. AD1 has 494K subs. AO has 274K subs. AD2 has 123K subs. Three very different subscriber counts. But right now, all three are averaging between 110K and 138K views per video. They have converged to nearly identical performance.

This convergence should be impossible if the problem were channel-specific. The only explanation is a shared root cause affecting all three equally. And what they share is: identical title formula, similar upload cadence, similar duration, same visual identity, and the same brand perception from the algorithm's perspective.

The algorithm doesn't see three channels. It sees one brand producing 20 identical-looking videos per month, and it has learned to throttle all of them.

**Failure Number Four: Universal Sequel Decay.**

Every channel shows the same pattern when topics are extended into volumes.

On AD1: Dashcam went from 1.19 million at Volume 1 to 61K at Volume 6 — a **95% decline**. CCTV went from 3.17 million to 132K at Volume 4 — a **96% decline**.

On AD2: Theme Park went from 1.19 million at its peak to 91K at Volume 5 — **92% decline**. Live TV from 791K to 107K — **87% decline**.

On AO: Talent Show Accidents from 455K at Volume 2 to 77K at Volume 3 — **83% decline**.

The average sequel decline across the entire network is **-75%**. By Volume 3 of any series, the topic has lost three-quarters of its audience. By Volume 5, it's lost over 90%. Yet the network continues producing Volume 4, 5, 6 — and created an entire channel, AD2, specifically to house these terminal sequels.

The one exception that proves the rule: AD2's "Things on Asian Internet" went FROM 603K at Volume 1 TO 1.17 million at Volume 2 — a **94% increase**. Why? Because the topic was genuinely novel. For every other series where the audience has already been captured, the numbering is pure downside.

**Failure Number Five: Unsustainable Duration.**

The competitive sweet spot for 1-million-plus-view outlier videos in this niche is about **28 minutes**. AD1 averages 59 minutes — more than double the sweet spot. AO averages 57 minutes.

The math on retention matters here. A viewer who watches 60% of a 35-minute video gives the same absolute watch time as a viewer who watches 35% of a 60-minute video — about 21 minutes either way. But YouTube promotes the higher retention percentage. The 35-minute video with 60% retention gets a stronger algorithmic signal than the 60-minute video with 35% retention, even though the raw watch time is identical.

**Failure Number Six: Topic Exhaustion.**

When you produce 20 videos per month across three channels in the same broad niche, you run out of A-tier topics fast. Compare the early topics — Footage Caught on Camera at 4 million, CCTV at 3.17 million, Internet Mysteries at 2.2 million — to the recent topics — Biblical Horrors at 60K, Amazon Incidents at 71K, Fishing Encounters at 65K, Chinese Shows at 54K.

The early topics tapped into universal experiences. The recent topics are niche, forced, and lack the visceral hook that drives mass-market clicks. This would happen naturally over time, but at 20 videos per month, the barrel was scraped 5 times faster than it needed to be.

### What I'd Change (2-3 minutes)

So those are the six systemic failures. Here's what I'd change — and all of these are tactical adjustments within the existing structure.

**First: Title diversification.** Keep the iceberg format as ONE of 4-5 title frameworks in rotation. Reserve "iceberg" for 1-2 genuinely deep, high-demand topics per month per channel — make it the premium offering instead of the default. Add numbered compilations, temporal hooks like "Best of Month," specific scenario titles, and resolution framing like "Solved" or "Explained." Each framework reaches a different audience segment. The content can be identical — the title determines who YouTube shows it to.

**Second: Reduce upload cadence from 20 per month network-wide to about 12** — 4 per channel. Every month with fewer uploads performed better on every channel. AD1 at 4 per month averaged 602K. At 8 per month, 142K. The math shows fewer videos would likely generate MORE total monthly views: 4 videos at 350K each equals 1.4 million monthly views, versus 8 videos at 128K each equals 1.02 million. Fewer videos, more total views, less production cost.

**Third: Kill visible sequel numbering.** Don't stop covering the same topics — stop labeling them as sequels. Instead of "Dashcam Footage Iceberg Vol. 6," try "The Dashcam Footage From Russia That Went Viral." Both can contain the same content. The second title doesn't tell new viewers they missed 5 episodes, gives YouTube specific keywords to match to specific audiences, and doesn't trigger "I already saw this" fatigue.

**Fourth: Optimize duration to 30-40 minutes for standard uploads.** Reserve 55-plus minutes only for quarterly premium event icebergs. A 7-level, 60-minute iceberg can become two 4-level, 30-minute videos — each with its own title, thumbnail, and audience targeting. More content opportunities at a length the algorithm rewards.

**Fifth: Sharpen each channel's lane.** AD1 owns real-world caught-on-camera content. AD2 builds its own identity around original content — the "Asian Internet" concept that grew 94% between volumes proves there's demand for regional and cultural deep dives. AO doubles down on named TV shows, which average 777K views compared to generic topics at 50K. When the algorithm sees three distinct products instead of one fragmented brand, it can recommend each one to the right audience.

### The Bottom Line on Q1 (30 seconds)

Abyssal's format is not aging. The format is being overproduced. The iceberg is still a genuinely superior structure — no competitor builds content with the same depth, escalation, or viewer investment. That's a real competitive advantage. The problem is that the network treated that advantage like a commodity and mass-produced it until the market couldn't absorb the volume.

The fix isn't to abandon the iceberg. The fix is to make the iceberg rare again — the event people show up for — and surround it with complementary formats that keep the channels active and discoverable.

## QUESTION 2: THREE VIDEO PITCHES (10-12 minutes)

Now, the three video pitches — one per channel. Each one is designed to address the specific strategic problems I just diagnosed, while staying within the existing channel structure and content lane.

Every topic I'm about to pitch has been verified as novel — not just across the 330 videos in the Abyssal Network, but across all of YouTube. No other creator has published an iceberg on any of these topics.

### PITCH 1: Abyssal Detective — "the Most Disturbing Hotels Iceberg" (3-4 minutes)

AD1's core problem is content exhaustion within the footage-type category. After 137 videos, the channel has covered every major camera type: CCTV — 4 volumes. Dashcam — 6 volumes. Bodycam — 2 volumes. Livestreams, drones, doorbell cameras, GoPro, camcorder — all done.

My pitch doesn't add another footage type. It opens a completely new dimension: location.

"the Most Disturbing Hotels Iceberg" organizes content by WHERE things happened, not what camera recorded them. This is a category shift that doubles the channel's content runway without changing anything about the brand.

**The framework:** 7 levels plus a Patreon-exclusive Level 8. 22 to 26 verified cases across the descent. 50-55 minutes — within AD1's proven sweet spot.

The iceberg descends thematically:
- Level 1-2 are the hotels everyone knows: the Cecil Hotel, the Elisa Lam case, the Las Vegas Mandalay Bay shooting — 60 dead, the deadliest mass shooting in modern US history. H.H. Holmes' Murder Castle from 1893.
- Level 3 is disasters: the Hyatt Regency walkway collapse — 114 dead, the MGM Grand fire — 85 dead, the Winecoff Hotel fire — 119 dead, marketed as "absolutely fireproof."
- Level 4 is hidden cameras: Erin Andrews filmed through a rigged peephole, winning a 55 million dollar verdict. A South Korean spy-cam ring across 42 rooms in 30 hotels.
- Level 5 is serial killers who used hotels as hunting grounds: Cottingham, the I-65 Killer, the Craigslist Killer.
- Level 6 is trafficking: the Days Inn 24 million dollar civil judgment, the nationwide hotel trafficking lawsuit estimated at 500 million to 1 billion.
- Level 7 is the forgotten cases that barely register in public memory.

Every single case is verified with named sources — fire marshal reports, engineering commissions, federal court records, police files.

**The hook:** Instead of the standard "I've searched the internet for the most disturbing" opening, this video opens with a sound — a hotel door clicking shut, the deadbolt turning, then silence. Then the narrator: "You check in, you lock the door, and you assume you're safe. But some hotel rooms have stories that the front desk will never tell you." It's 30 seconds, it's personal — every viewer has heard that sound — and it previews serial killers, fires, and hidden cameras to create forward pull through the descent.

**What makes it different:** It opens a "location iceberg" lane. If this video performs, AD1 now has a second content dimension — airports, hospitals, national parks, schools. Each is a location with enough documented incidents for a full iceberg. The channel can produce icebergs by footage type AND by location, doubling the content runway.

**Novelty verification:** I searched "hotel iceberg," "hotels iceberg," "motel iceberg," and "disturbing hotel iceberg" on YouTube. Zero results. No creator on YouTube has published a structured hotel iceberg. Individual hotel videos exist — Cecil Hotel documentaries, Mandalay Bay coverage — but nobody has organized the full spectrum into one descending iceberg. AD1 would be the first.

### PITCH 2: Abyssal Detective 2 — "the Most Disturbing Elevator & Escalator Incidents Iceberg" (3-4 minutes)

AD2's problem is identity. Its own channel description says it exists to post "Vol. 3+ videos" — it was literally created as a sequel dump. 95% of its 80 videos are sequels of AD1 series. The only time it produced original content — "Things on Asian Internet" — it became the channel's only series to GROW between volumes, from 602K to 1.17 million, a 94% increase.

This pitch gives AD2 original content that proves the channel can stand on its own.

**The topic:** Across all 330 videos in the Abyssal network, the words "elevator," "escalator," and "lift" appear zero times. Yet elevators and escalators have near-universal CCTV coverage, they're universally relatable — everyone uses them — and the incidents range from viral malfunctions to mass-casualty disasters.

**The framework:** 7 levels, 20-24 cases, and critically — **30-35 minutes**, not 48. AD2's current average is 48 minutes at 7-8 uploads per month, asking viewers for about 6 hours per month. At 30-35 minutes and 4-5 uploads per month, that drops to about 2.5 hours — closer to sustainable.

The iceberg descends through machine failure severity:
- Level 1 is malfunctions — the Rome Metro escalator that accelerated and injured 20 people.
- Level 3 is close calls — the Chile elevator that rocketed 31 floors in 15 seconds.
- Level 4 is the Jingzhou escalator death in China — the mother who pushed her son to safety as the floor panel swallowed her. That clip alone has billions of impressions across all platforms.
- Level 5 is fatal failures — Samuel Waisbren, 30, crushed in a New York elevator that had 16 violations on record. Jaumarcus McFarland, 18, killed in an Atlanta elevator that was more than a year overdue for inspection.
- Level 7 is the World Trade Center — an estimated 200 people died in the elevators on September 11, the deadliest elevator disaster in history.

**The hook:** "You step onto one 20 times a week without thinking. You trust it with your life without knowing it. And when something goes wrong inside a metal box suspended by cables above an empty shaft — there is nowhere to run, and almost always — a camera watching everything."

**What makes it different:** This is not a sequel. It has no volume number. It creates a territory for AD2 — niche incident categories that AD1's broader format doesn't cover. If it works, AD2 has a lane: elevator incidents, subway incidents, construction site incidents — specific machines and environments that failed. That's an identity. Not "AD1's leftovers."

**Novelty verification:** Searched "elevator iceberg" and "escalator iceberg" on YouTube. Zero iceberg results. Individual news clips exist — the Chile elevator has over 1 million views as a 72-second CBS clip — but nobody has done the structured deep dive.

### PITCH 3: Abyssal Observer — "the Most Disturbing Kitchen Nightmares Iceberg" (3-4 minutes)

AO's problem is the opposite of AD1's. AO has the clearest niche position in the network — TV and entertainment icebergs. It has the highest RPM at $7.72 and the most revenue at nearly $27,000 per month. But recently, it's been drifting from what works into obscure territory.

The data is unmistakable. Named-show icebergs on AO — shows like TLC, To Catch a Predator, My Strange Addiction, Intervention — average **777,000 views**. Recent generic topic icebergs — Marvel, Anime Based on Real Events, Chinese Shows, Directors Behind Famous Films — average **50,000 views**. That's a **15.6x performance gap**. Named shows with large existing fanbases win. Generic topics with audiences that need to be built lose.

This pitch doubles down on the strategy that made AO successful.

**Why Kitchen Nightmares:** The official Kitchen Nightmares YouTube channel has over **10 million subscribers**. Individual clips routinely hit 10-50 million views. Amy's Baking Company alone has been viewed hundreds of millions of times. It is one of the most algorithmically proven reality shows on YouTube.

Yet across all 330 Abyssal Network videos, the words "Kitchen Nightmares," "Kitchen," "Gordon Ramsay," "Hell's Kitchen," and "MasterChef" appear **zero times**. Zero coverage of the most popular reality TV franchise on YouTube, from a channel whose entire identity is reality TV icebergs.

And there's no Kitchen Nightmares iceberg anywhere else on YouTube either. I searched "Kitchen Nightmares iceberg" and "Gordon Ramsay iceberg." Zero results.

**The framework:** 7 levels, 45-50 minutes, within AO's proven sweet spot. The iceberg descends from entertainment to exploitation:
- Level 1 is the greatest hits — Amy's Baking Company, the iconic Ramsay moments everyone quotes.
- Level 2 is the numbers: **82% of featured restaurants have closed**. Gordon's "rescues" almost never work.
- Level 3 is production manipulation — the Dillons restaurant manager who filed a 3 million dollar lawsuit alleging producers planted spoiled meat and rigged a chair to collapse. The UK version versus the US version — fundamentally different shows.
- Level 4 is criminal records — Samy Bouzaglo was deported for undisclosed drug trafficking and extortion convictions. Amy had previously served time for identity theft.
- Level 5 is the deaths — Joe Cerniglia, owner of Campania, who Ramsay told "your business is about to swim down the Hudson," died by suicide three years later.
- Level 6-7 examines the system itself: a show that targeted financially distressed restaurant owners, subjected them to public humiliation, broadcast their worst moments to millions — and produced an 82% failure rate.

**The hook:** Open with the Amy's Baking Company walkout clip — the moment everyone knows — then freeze frame, silence, and: "You've seen the screaming. You've seen the rotten food. But there are things about Kitchen Nightmares that the show never aired, that the producers never talked about, and that the audience was never supposed to find out."

**What makes it different:** This video has built-in emotional variety that most icebergs lack. You start by laughing at Amy's Baking Company. You end by questioning whether you were part of the problem. That arc — comedy to surprise to suspicion to shock to grief to moral reckoning — is what separates a viral video from a meaningful one. And meaningful videos get shared differently.

**A critical production note for Level 5:** The deaths section must be handled with restraint. Music drops to near-silence, narration slows, no sensationalist language. This isn't just an ethical choice — it's a retention choice. A sudden tonal shift at the 30-minute mark resets the viewer's attention and buys another 15-20 minutes of retained engagement because they can't predict what comes next.

## CLOSING (1 minute)

To summarize: the Abyssal Network isn't failing because the format stopped working. It's failing because it scaled the format like a factory — more volume, faster output, identical packaging — in a medium that rewards the opposite: scarcity, variation, and surprise.

The five changes I'd make — title diversification, reduced upload cadence, shorter standard duration, standalone titles, and sharper channel lanes — are all tactical. None require abandoning the iceberg, merging channels, or rebranding. They work WITH the existing structure to reverse what overproduction created.

And the three video pitches I've presented — Hotels for AD1, Elevators for AD2, Kitchen Nightmares for AO — each address the specific strategic problem their channel faces while opening new content lanes for the future. Every topic is verified as novel across YouTube. Every case is real and sourced. And every recommendation stays within the existing channel identity.

Thank you for considering my application.
