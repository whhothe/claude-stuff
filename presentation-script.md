## INTRO — 1.5 min

Hi, I'm [YOUR NAME], and this is my application for the Creative Strategist role at NIA
Production.

Two questions were asked. Where is the Abyssal Network failing — brand-wide and per channel
— and why are baseline views falling. And second, three new video ideas, one per channel.

I went through all **330 videos** across the three channels, the month-by-month performance
of each one, and about two dozen competitors in the same niche.

One thing before I start. When I pulled the numbers apart, one of my own early conclusions
didn't survive. I'm going to tell you about that when I get to it, because I think how
someone handles a finding that contradicts them is more useful to you than a clean story.

Let's start with the diagnosis.

## QUESTION 1 — 11 min

### First, what is *not* wrong — 1.5 min

*[SLIDE: niche-is-thriving.png]*

The niche is not dying. A channel called **SIRIUS** — 300K subscribers, **18 videos total**
— is averaging **2.8 million views a video**. Chilling Scares, at 2.86 million subs, is
holding a **318K Day-1 median** and still growing around 18% a year. A channel called
Absolute Chaos Moments, with **1,550 subscribers** — not a typo — put up a video that did
**1.19 million**. People want this content right now.

And the audience hasn't turned on Abyssal either. The like-to-view ratio across the three
channels is flat or up — AD1 went from **1.86% in 2024 to 2.91% this year**. The people who
find these videos like them slightly *more* than they used to.

So the format works, the niche is hot, and the audience is happy. What's broken is reach.
Fewer people are being shown the videos. That's the thing to explain.

### The headline — 1 min

*[SLIDE: the-collapse.png]*

Here's the scale of it. AD1 peaked at just over **2 million average views** in March 2025.
It's at **139,000** now — down **93%**. Abyssal Observer peaked at **1.39 million** in
February 2026 and is at **141,000** — down **90%**. AD2 went from **601,000** to
**133,000** — down **78%**.

Eighteen months, and the biggest channel lost 93% of its per-video reach. That's not drift.
That's a structural problem.

### What's causing it — 5.5 min

*[SLIDE: title-monoculture.png]*

**The packaging is the biggest single problem.**

When I audited the titles, **321 of 328 videos** were "the Most Disturbing [something]
Iceberg." That's **98%**. Two more videos have gone up since I ran that count, so call it
98% of 330 today.

Here's why that matters mechanically. YouTube uses the title to work out *who* to show a
video to. When 321 videos share one structure, it can't tell them apart. It has no way to
know that a dashcam iceberg should reach a different person than a CCTV iceberg. So it
defaults to serving both to the same pool — the people who clicked the last one. That pool
only shrinks.

And on the viewer side, a subscriber seeing "Most Disturbing [X] Iceberg" for the fiftieth
time stops registering it as new. Not because the video is worse. Because the title is
identical to the last forty-nine.

Compare that to who's winning. Chilling Scares runs four or five title frameworks. GOHA,
DICA, MachRush all run six or more. Their big videos are titled things like "120 Incredible
Moments Caught on Camera" — **6.78 million** — or "When Truckers Put An End To Road Madness"
— **2.57 million**. You don't need to know what an iceberg is to understand those.

And Abyssal has already run the experiment. Exactly one video on AD1 broke the formula —
"4chan Threads That Triggered FBI Investigations." It did **407,000**, which is roughly
three times the channel's current average. One title change.

*[SLIDE: convergence.png]*

**Second — the three channels have converged, and that tells you the cause is shared.**

AD1 has **494,000** subscribers. Abyssal Observer has **274,000**. AD2 has **123,000**.
Four times the subscriber spread between the biggest and smallest.

They're now averaging **138,000**, **127,000**, and **110,000** views. Essentially the same
number.

That shouldn't be possible if these were three separate problems. A channel with 494K subs
should have a meaningfully higher floor than one with 123K. The only thing that explains
three different channels landing on one floor is a cause they share — same title formula,
same length, same thumbnails, same brand fingerprint.

The algorithm isn't seeing three channels. It's seeing one publisher putting out eighteen
near-identical videos a month, and it's throttling the whole thing.

*[SLIDE: sequel-decay.png]*

**Third — sequels decay, without exception.**

Every multi-volume series I could measure declines from its best volume to its most recent.
Dashcam: **1.19 million** down to **61,000** by Volume 6 — **−95%**. CCTV: **3.17 million**
to **132,000** by Volume 4 — **−96%**. Theme Park on AD2, **−92%**. Live TV, **−87%**.
Talent Show Accidents on AO, **−83%**.

Seven series, declines from 50% to 96%, averaging **−85%**.

There's one exception, and it's the interesting one. AD2's "Things on Asian Internet" went
*up* between volumes — **602,000 to 1.17 million**, a **94% increase**. The difference is
that the topic was genuinely new. Everywhere else, Volume 4 is being sold to an audience
that already got the best material in Volume 1.

*[SLIDE: the-volume-question.png]*

**Fourth — and this is the one where I have to correct myself.**

My first read was that the network was uploading too much and cannibalising itself. It's
the obvious story: uploads went up, views went down. I was ready to recommend cutting output
by 40%.

Then I controlled for time, and it mostly fell apart.

Across all 61 channel-months, the correlation between upload count and per-video views is
only **−0.21** — weak. And if you look at **2026 alone**, so that you're comparing months in
the same conditions, it drops to **−0.13**. Months with five or fewer uploads averaged
**284,952** views a video. Months with seven or more averaged **282,666**. That's the same
number. On Abyssal Observer the correlation actually goes slightly *positive*.

What was really happening is that the early months were both low-cadence *and* early — before
the topics were used up and the formula got stale. Cadence was riding along with time; it
wasn't driving much.

And the recommendation I nearly made would have cost money. In 2026, months with seven or
more uploads produced **2.05 million** total views. Months with five or fewer produced
**1.22 million**. More videos genuinely does mean more total views — roughly 1.7x the views
for 1.7x the videos. Telling you to cut output would have cut revenue.

But there *is* a real finding in there, and it's sharper than the one I started with.

*[SLIDE: the-eight-video-cliff.png]*

Break 2026 out by exact upload count. At **six** uploads a month the network averages **2.07
million** total views. At **seven**, **3.0 million** — the best tier there is. At **eight**,
it falls off a cliff to **1.09 million**.

Seven works. Eight breaks. Per-video views at eight uploads drop to **136,000**, less than a
third of the seven-upload tier.

So the recommendation isn't "publish less." It's **"never publish eight."** Cap each channel
at seven. That's a much smaller ask than what I was originally going to tell you, and unlike
the original it's actually supported.

*[SLIDE: duration.png]*

**Fifth — length.** The videos in this niche that clear a million views cluster around **28
minutes**. AD1 averages **59**. AO averages **57**. That's over double.

The retention maths matters here. Someone watching 60% of a 35-minute video and someone
watching 35% of a 60-minute video both give you about 21 minutes. Identical watch time. But
YouTube rewards the *percentage*, so the shorter video gets the better signal for the same
minutes delivered.

Stack that across the network and you're asking your audience for roughly **17 hours of
video a month**. Chilling Scares asks for about **1.1**. You're asking for fifteen times
more of someone's life than your nearest competitor.

**Sixth — the topics are getting thinner.** The early hits were universal: Footage Caught on
Camera at **4 million**, CCTV at **3.17 million**, Internet Mysteries at **2.2 million**.
The recent ones are Biblical Horrors at **60,000**, Amazon Incidents at **71,000**, Chinese
Shows at **54,000**. That drift happens to every channel eventually. At eighteen videos a
month it happened about five times faster than it had to.

### Per channel — 2.5 min

*[SLIDE: per-channel.png]*

Brand-wide is most of it, but each channel has its own version.

**Abyssal Detective** has the steepest fall — **−93%** — because it's the one that ran out of
its organising principle. AD1 sorts content by *what recorded it*. CCTV, four volumes.
Dashcam, six. Bodycam, doorbell, drone, GoPro, livestream, camcorder — all used. After 137
videos there isn't a camera type left. It's also the longest channel at 59 minutes, so it's
asking the most and has the least new to offer.

**Abyssal Detective 2** has an identity problem, and it's structural. The channel
description says it exists to post "Vol. 3+ videos." **95% of its 80 videos are sequels of
AD1 series.** It was built as an overflow pipe, so it inherited AD1's decay curve without
ever building an audience of its own. And the single time it made something original, that
became the only thing in the network that grew between volumes. That's not a coincidence.

**Abyssal Observer** is the healthiest and the most fixable. It has the best economics —
**$7.72 RPM**, about **$26,700 a month**, the most in the network — and the clearest lane:
named TV shows. The problem is it's drifting off that lane. Its named-show icebergs — TLC,
To Catch a Predator, Intervention, My Strange Addiction, 90 Day Fiancé, Cheapskates —
average **777,000 views**. Its recent generic ones — Marvel, Anime Based on Real Events,
Chinese Shows, Directors Behind Famous Films — average **50,000**. That's a **15.6x** gap,
and it's conservative, because I left the two biggest named-show videos out of the average.
AO's decline is the easiest to reverse because it's mostly a choice about what to cover.

### What I'd change — 2.5 min

*[SLIDE: five-changes.png]*

Five changes. All of them work inside the current structure — nothing gets merged, renamed,
or shut down.

**One. Break the title monoculture.** Keep the iceberg as one of four or five frameworks
rather than the only one. Reserve "Iceberg" for one or two genuinely deep topics a month per
channel and let it become the premium thing instead of the default. Add numbered
compilations, specific-scenario titles, "Solved" and "Explained" framings. The content
doesn't have to change at all — the title decides who gets shown it.

**Two. Cap at seven uploads per channel per month.** Not a cut in output — a ceiling. Seven
is the best-performing tier in the data and eight is where it collapses. This is close to
free.

**Three. Drop the visible volume numbers.** Keep covering the same topics — just stop
labelling them as sequels. "Dashcam Footage Iceberg Vol. 6" becomes "The Dashcam Footage
From Russia That Nobody Can Explain." Same video. But it doesn't announce to a new viewer
that they've missed five episodes, and it gives YouTube specific keywords to match instead
of a series it already knows is declining.

**Four. Bring standard uploads to 30–40 minutes** and save 55-plus for a quarterly event
iceberg. A seven-level, 60-minute video can be two 30-minute videos with their own titles
and thumbnails, hitting two audiences instead of one.

**Five. Give each channel a real lane.** AD1 owns caught-on-camera. AD2 owns original
standalone topics — its own data says that's the one thing that grows there. AO goes back to
named shows, where the gap is 15x. Three distinct products are three things the algorithm
can route to three audiences. One blurred brand is one thing it can throttle.

### The bottom line — 30 sec

The format isn't tired. The format is over-packaged. The iceberg is genuinely a better
structure than anything the competition is building — nobody else does this depth or this
escalation. That's a real advantage. It just got treated like a commodity and printed until
the market stopped noticing it.

Make the iceberg the event again, and put other formats around it.

## QUESTION 2 — THREE PITCHES — 10 min

*[SLIDE: three-pitches.png]*

One per channel, each aimed at the specific problem that channel has. And each of these was
checked twice: nothing like it exists in the 330 Abyssal videos, and nothing like it exists
anywhere on YouTube.

### Pitch 1 — Abyssal Detective: "the Most Disturbing Hotels Iceberg" — 3.5 min

*[SLIDE: pitch-hotels.png]*

AD1's problem is that it's out of camera types. So I'm not proposing another one. I'm
proposing a different axis entirely: **location**.

Organise by *where it happened* instead of *what filmed it*, and the channel has a whole
second dimension of content. Hotels first — then airports, hospitals, national parks,
schools. Every one of those has enough documented material for a full iceberg. That's the
real value of this pitch: it's not one video, it's a lane.

**The framework.** Seven levels plus a Patreon Level 8. **22 to 26 verified cases.** 50 to
55 minutes.

The descent is thematic, not just severity:

- **Levels 1–2** are the ones people came for — the Cecil Hotel and Elisa Lam, Mandalay Bay
  and the 60 people killed in 2017, H.H. Holmes' Murder Castle.
- **Level 3** is disaster: the Hyatt Regency walkway collapse, **114 dead**. The MGM Grand
  fire, **85**. The Winecoff in Atlanta, **119** — a hotel that advertised itself as
  absolutely fireproof.
- **Level 4** is surveillance. Erin Andrews, filmed through a modified peephole at the
  Nashville Marriott, **$55 million verdict** in 2016. And a South Korean ring that put
  cameras in **42 rooms across 30 hotels** and livestreamed roughly **1,600 guests** to more
  than **4,000 paying subscribers**.
- **Level 5** is hotels as hunting grounds — Richard Cottingham, the I-65 Killer, the
  Craigslist Killer.
- **Level 6** is trafficking, and this is where it stops being history. A Days Inn in
  Philadelphia paid **$24 million** to eight victims in 2023. A Georgia jury returned
  **$40 million** in 2025 for a woman trafficked at sixteen. Three more Philadelphia hotels
  settled for **$17.5 million** this year. This is an active, ongoing liability across the
  industry.
- **Level 7** is the cases nobody remembers.

Every one of those is sourced to a fire marshal report, an engineering commission, or a
court record. I'm not using anything I couldn't hand you the citation for.

**The hook.** Not "I've searched the internet for the most disturbing." It opens on sound —
a hotel door clicking shut, the deadbolt turning, then nothing. Then: *"You check in, you
lock the door, and you assume that's the end of it. Some of these rooms have a history the
front desk will never mention."* Thirty seconds. It works because every single viewer has
heard that exact sound and felt momentarily safe because of it.

**Why it beats the original format.** A standard AD1 video promises footage. This promises
a place the viewer has personally been. That's a different and stronger kind of dread —
it survives the video ending.

**Novelty.** I searched hotel iceberg, hotels iceberg, motel iceberg, disturbing hotel
iceberg. **Zero results.** Cecil Hotel documentaries exist, Mandalay Bay coverage exists —
nobody has assembled the whole spectrum into one descent. AD1 would be first.

### Pitch 2 — Abyssal Detective 2: "the Most Disturbing Elevator & Escalator Incidents Iceberg" — 3 min

*[SLIDE: pitch-elevators.png]*

AD2's problem is that it has never been allowed to be its own channel. So this pitch has no
volume number and no parent series on AD1. That's the point of it.

Across all 330 videos in the network, the words elevator, escalator, and lift appear **zero
times**. And it's a natural fit — these machines have near-total CCTV coverage, so footage
essentially always exists, and everybody on earth uses them without thinking.

**The framework.** Seven levels, **20 to 24 cases**, and deliberately **30 to 35 minutes**
rather than AD2's current 48. Testing the shorter format on the channel that can most afford
to experiment.

The descent runs on how badly the machine failed:

- **Level 1** is malfunction — the Rome Metro escalator in 2018 that accelerated and injured
  around twenty people.
- **Level 3** is the near-miss. José Vergara Acevedo in Santiago, Chile — his elevator
  climbed **31 floors in 15 seconds** and hit the roof at about 80 kilometres an hour. He
  lived. The CCTV is extraordinary.
- **Level 4** is Jingzhou, 2015 — the mother who pushed her son clear as the escalator's
  floor panel gave way beneath her, and didn't get out herself.
- **Level 5** is negligence. Samuel Waisbren, 30, killed in a Manhattan elevator in 2019 —
  the building had been fined months earlier because the door-zone restrictor, the exact
  safety device that stops the doors opening while the car is moving, had been disabled. It
  was still unfixed the day he died. And JauMarcus McFarland, 18, killed in Atlanta in an
  elevator whose operating permit had expired the previous August, overloaded past its
  3,000-pound limit.
- **Level 7** is the World Trade Center. USA Today's investigation estimated roughly **200
  people died inside the elevators** on September 11th — the deadliest elevator disaster in
  history.

**The hook.** *"You step into one maybe twenty times a week and never think about it. A
metal box hanging over an empty shaft, and you get in without looking. When it goes wrong
there's nowhere to go — and there's almost always a camera."*

**Why it works for this channel specifically.** It gives AD2 a territory: specific machines
and environments that failed. Elevators, then subways, then construction sites, then
industrial accidents. That's an identity. Right now the channel's identity is "the place
Volume 5 goes."

**Novelty.** Elevator iceberg, escalator iceberg — **zero results**. The individual clips
are huge; the Chile footage alone has over a million views as a 72-second news segment.
Nobody has done the structured version.

### Pitch 3 — Abyssal Observer: "the Most Disturbing Kitchen Nightmares Iceberg" — 3.5 min

*[SLIDE: pitch-kitchen-nightmares.png]*

AO's problem isn't exhaustion — it's drift. The 15.6x gap I showed earlier is the whole
argument. Named shows work on this channel. Generic topics don't. So this pitch goes back to
what works, with the biggest untapped name available.

**Why this one.** The official Kitchen Nightmares channel has over **10 million
subscribers** and individual clips routinely do tens of millions of views. It's one of the
most algorithmically proven properties on YouTube.

And across all 330 Abyssal videos, Kitchen Nightmares, Gordon Ramsay, Hell's Kitchen and
MasterChef appear **zero times**. A channel built on reality TV icebergs has never touched
the biggest reality franchise on the platform. There's also no Kitchen Nightmares iceberg
anywhere else on YouTube — I checked that too. **Zero results.**

**The framework.** Seven levels, 45 to 50 minutes, **16 to 20 cases**. It descends from
comedy into something much heavier:

- **Level 1** is what everyone came for — Amy's Baking Company, the famous walkouts.
- **Level 2** is the number that reframes the show: by the most-cited tracking count,
  **more than 80% of the restaurants featured have since closed.** The rescues mostly
  didn't work.
- **Level 3** is manufacture. Martin Hyde, the general manager from the Dillons episode,
  sued for **$3 million**, alleging producers used rotten meat, rigged a chair to collapse,
  and hired actors to pose as customers. Worth saying plainly: **he lost, and the episode
  aired.** The allegation is the story, not a proven fact.
- **Level 4** is the criminal records behind the famous episode. Samy Bouzaglo was deported
  over overseas extortion and drug convictions he hadn't disclosed. Amy — who had previously
  gone by Amanda Bossingham — served **14 months in federal prison** for using another
  person's Social Security number to apply for a $15,000 line of credit.
- **Level 5** is Joe Cerniglia. He owned Campania, Ramsay told him on camera that his
  business was about to swim down the Hudson, and three years later, in 2010, he died by
  suicide off the George Washington Bridge into that river. I'd handle this carefully and
  say on screen what's true: **his family have said his time on the show was a good period
  for him.** The video presents the sequence and doesn't claim the cause. That restraint is
  the right call ethically, and it's also just more credible.
- **Levels 6–7** turn on the format itself — a show that sought out people in financial
  crisis, filmed their worst week, and posted an 80%-plus failure rate.

**The hook.** Open on the Amy's Baking Company walkout — the clip everybody knows — then cut
it dead. Silence. *"You've seen the screaming and you've seen the raw chicken. What didn't
air is who some of these people already were, and what happened to them after the cameras
left."*

**Why it beats a standard AO video.** Emotional range. Most icebergs run one note — dread —
for fifty minutes. This one starts as comedy, turns uncomfortable, then turns genuinely
sad, then asks the viewer a real question about their own enjoyment of it. That arc is what
gets a video sent to someone rather than just watched.

**One production note.** The tonal drop at Level 5 isn't only an ethics call. A hard change
of register thirty minutes in resets attention and buys back another fifteen or twenty
minutes of retention, because the viewer can no longer predict the shape of what's coming.

---

## CLOSING — 1 min

So: the network isn't failing because the format got old. It's failing because it was
scaled like a factory — same packaging, more units, faster — in a medium that pays for
scarcity and surprise.

The five changes are all tactical. Nothing gets merged, rebranded, or shut down. And the
three pitches each go at the specific thing wrong with their channel — Hotels gives AD1 a
second axis, Elevators gives AD2 its first identity, Kitchen Nightmares puts AO back on the
thing that pays.

And on the volume point — I'd rather show you the analysis that changed my mind than a
tidier story that doesn't hold. If I'm in this role, that's how I'd want to bring you
findings.

Thank you for your time.
