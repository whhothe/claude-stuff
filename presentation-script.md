# QUESTION 1 — WHERE ARE WE FAILING

## Pass one: the brand — 8 min

### The number — 1 min

*[SLIDE: the-collapse.png]*

Abyssal Detective peaked at just over **two million average views** in March 2025. It's at
**139,000**. That's **minus 93 percent**.

Observer peaked at **1.39 million** in February 2026 and is at **141,000** — **minus 90**.
Detective 2, **601,000** down to **133,000** — **minus 78**.

Eighteen months. The flagship lost 93 percent of its per-video reach. Nothing in the format

### What it is not — 45 sec

Two comfortable answers, both wrong, and I want them off the table before I give you mine.

**It's not the titles.** 321 of 328 videos use the formula — 98 percent. But look at the peaks:
*Footage Caught on Camera* at 4.03 million, *CCTV Incidents* at 3.17 million. Same formula. The
titles were identical when the channel was doing two million a video. A constant can't explain a
change. Titles are a real problem, but they're a ceiling problem, not the cause of the fall, and
I'll come back to them.

**And it's not volume.** That was my first answer and it didn't survive.

*[SLIDE: the-volume-question.png]*

Across all 61 channel-months the correlation between uploads and views is **minus 0.21**. Weak.
Restrict it to 2026, so you're comparing like conditions, and it's **minus 0.13**. Months with
five or fewer uploads averaged **284,952** views a video. Months with seven or more averaged
**282,666**. That's the same number. On Observer the correlation is slightly *positive*.

What was really happening is that the early months were both low-cadence and early. Cadence was
riding along with time. I was about to recommend cutting output by forty percent and it would
have cost you money — in 2026, higher-cadence months produced **2.05 million** total views
against **1.22 million** for low-cadence ones. More videos really does mean more total views.

There's a real finding inside that, and it's sharper than the one I started with. I'll get to it.

### What it actually is — 2.5 min

*[SLIDE: curiosity-to-close.png]*

Your own premise doc says what the product is. *"Abyssal tells real life horror stories so the
audience never has to go and find them… the viewer gets their morbid curiosity closed just by
sitting and watching."*

That sentence contains the failure. **A topic only works if there is pre-existing morbid
curiosity to close.** You are not in the business of making people curious. You are in the
business of discharging curiosity that already exists.

Now look at what the catalogue did over eighteen months.

The early topics all had it built in. *Footage Caught on Camera* — 4.03 million. *CCTV* — 3.17
million. *Internet Mysteries* — 2.2 million. Every one of those is something a person has
already half-wondered about.

The recent ones don't. *Biblical Horrors* — 60,000. *Amazon Incidents* — 71,000. *Fishing
Encounters* — 65,000. *Chinese Shows* — 54,000. Nobody walks around with unresolved morbid
curiosity about fishing. There was no itch, so nothing got scratched.

And you already have the instrument that catches this. The brief lists three tests every topic
must pass: **is there real depth, does the surface pull a click from someone who's never heard
of you, and does the descent go somewhere the audience can't easily find on their own.** All
three, yes.

Run the recent slate through it. *Fishing Encounters* fails test one and test three. *Amazon
Incidents* fails one and three. *Biblical Horrors* fails all three and brushes the religion
line in your own gray-zone rule.

The filter exists. It isn't being applied. That is the whole diagnosis, and everything else is
downstream of it.

### Why the filter stopped being applied — 1.5 min

*[SLIDE: the-35-case-trap.png]*

This is the part I'd push back on hardest, and it's my answer to what you're protecting for the
wrong reasons.

**The 35-to-50-case, 45-to-60-minute requirement is manufacturing your topic problem.**

Do the arithmetic the greenlight actually faces. Seven levels, five to seven cases each, every
single case needing thirty-plus seconds of usable footage and a sixty-to-120-second story. To
clear that you need a topic broad enough to yield fifty separate filmed incidents.

Almost nothing with genuine morbid curiosity attached is that broad. So the filter inverts.
Instead of "what do people already want to know," the question silently becomes "what can I find
fifty clips of." And *Fishing Encounters* is what that question returns.

You are not protecting the iceberg. The iceberg is genuinely good — nobody else builds this
depth. **You're protecting the runtime**, and the runtime is picking your topics for you.

### The rest of it — 2 min

Four more, quickly, all real but all secondary to that.

*[SLIDE: sequel-decay.png]*

**Sequels.** Seven multi-volume series, decline from best volume to latest between 50 and 96
percent, averaging **minus 85**. CCTV, minus 96. Dashcam, minus 95. One exception, and it's the
one that proves the point — *Things on Asian Internet* went **up** 94 percent between volumes,
because the topic was actually new. The brief makes "which series earn a next volume and which
are exhausted" the strategist's call. That call isn't being made.

*[SLIDE: the-eight-video-cliff.png]*

**Eight uploads.** Here's the real finding from the volume analysis. Break 2026 out by exact
upload count. Six uploads a month: 2.07 million total views. Seven: **3.01 million**, the best
tier there is. Eight: **1.09 million**. Per-video at seven is 429,000. At eight it's 136,000.

Seven works. Eight breaks. And your premise doc says each channel uploads about twice a week —
which is eight a month. **Your stated cadence is precisely the tier where the numbers
collapse.** That's not a call to publish less. It's a call to never publish eight.

*[SLIDE: convergence.png]*

**Convergence.** 494,000 subscribers, 274,000, and 123,000 — and average views of 138, 127 and
110 thousand. Four times the audience spread, one identical floor. Three channels can't land on
one number by coincidence. It's one shared cause, and it's the one I just described.

*[SLIDE: the-ask.png]*

**And the size of the ask.** Three channels, about eighteen and a half videos a month, at a
weighted average of fifty-five minutes. That's **roughly seventeen hours of video a month** from

The retention maths is the part that matters. Sixty percent of a 35-minute video and thirty-five
percent of a 60-minute video are the same twenty-one minutes watched — but YouTube rewards the

**And titles — as a ceiling, not a cause.** 98 percent identical. The single video that broke

## What I'd change — 2.5 min

*[SLIDE: five-changes.png]*

**One. Enforce the three-test filter at greenlight, in writing.** It already exists. Nothing
gets a slot without all three yeses, and the "no" gets written down with the reason. That alone
would have stopped *Fishing Encounters*.

**Two. Cap at seven uploads per channel. Never eight.** Not a cut — a ceiling, one notch below
your current cadence, on the tier where the data breaks.

**Three. Kill visible volume numbers, starting with AD2.** Same topics, standalone titles.
"Dashcam Vol. 5" becomes "The Dashcam Footage From Russia Nobody Can Explain." Minus 85 percent
is what the number costs you.

**Four. Put three title frameworks into rotation** and reserve "Iceberg" for one or two premium
uploads per channel per month, so it means something again.

**Five. Change what a level means.** This is the one that matters, and I'll spend Q2 on it
rather than describing it twice — so here it is in one sentence. Right now a level is a
severity tier, which is why the videos need fifty clips to feel deep. Make the level about
**what was written down before it happened** instead, and the depth stops coming from volume and
starts coming from the record. All three of my pitches are that same change applied three
times.

### And the thing I'd want to test, once I'd earned it — 45 sec

*[SLIDE: next-format.png]*

You said the strategist has to research the format that comes next, so I'll tell you where I'd
look — but I want to be straight about the standing. I have never shipped a video on your
channels. I have your public numbers and nothing else. So this is a hypothesis I'd want to test
cheaply, not a recommendation I'd make on day one.

**One case. Seven levels. Twenty-five to thirty-five minutes.** Same descent, but depth means
*further into one incident* rather than *more incidents*. Level 1 is what the public knows,
Level 7 is what's in the primary record nobody has read.

Why I'd want to find out: it removes the fifty-clip constraint entirely, it lands near the
28-minute mark where this niche's outliers cluster, and the deep levels are something a solo
creator structurally cannot produce.

Why I wouldn't bet the slate on it: lower case density means if the single case doesn't hold,
there's nothing else carrying the video. That's a real risk and I can't price it from outside.

So: one video, one channel, one month, measured against that channel's own baseline. If it
works, it scales. If it doesn't, you've lost one upload slot and learned something.

# QUESTION 2 — THREE PITCHES

## First: what makes it an Abyssal video — 1 min

*[SLIDE: five-invariants.png]*

You listed five things that are true of every Abyssal video. Real and verified. Disturbing but
survivable, because the guide takes the hit. **Forbidden or obscure — something the viewer could
not or should not find alone.** A moral frame. A human guide.

I'd argue the third one is the product and the other four are quality control on it. The moment
a topic is something the viewer could comfortably find alone, you've stopped closing morbid
curiosity and started competing on production value against people with more time than you.

Which is a problem, because I checked, and almost every subject with real morbid curiosity
attached to it already has a video with millions of views on it. Kitchen Nightmares, child
stars, India, Telegram — I tested all of them. Every one is taken. **You cannot win this on
topic novelty any more.** That era is over.

So I'm not going to pitch you three untouched subjects, because I don't believe they exist. I'm
going to pitch you one change to the format, applied three times.

## The change: every segment ends on the paperwork — 2 min

*[SLIDE: the-document-axis.png]*

Right now a level is a severity tier. Level 1 is bad, Level 7 is worse. That's why the videos
feel like lists, and it's why they need fifty clips — severity is the only thing stacking, so
you need volume to make the stack tall.

The change is this: **every single segment ends on the document.** Not the footage, not the
death toll — the piece of paper that already existed. The inspection that was failed. The report
that was filed. The background check that was run and ignored.

I want to be precise about what that does and doesn't change, because I got this wrong once
already and had to back it out.

My first version made the *level* mean the document — Level 1 nobody knew, Level 7 they knew and
signed it off anyway. It reads beautifully and it doesn't survive arithmetic. Your spec needs
**five to seven cases per level**, and "they knew and did it anyway" is the rarest state there
is. You can find one Mottarone. You cannot find six of them at the bottom of every topic.

So the levels still escalate on harm, which is abundant at every tier. The document is the
**signature of every case**, not the definition of the level. Same effect, and it actually
fills.

Five things it buys you.

**It fixes the obscurity problem.** The surface can be completely familiar — everybody has seen
Surfside come down. The depth isn't the footage, it's the report. Inquiry findings, inspection
histories and court files genuinely are not on YouTube. That's the last place where "the viewer
could not find this alone" is still literally true — and I'd argue topic novelty is finished as
a strategy. I tested Kitchen Nightmares, child stars, India and Telegram. Every one already has
a video with millions of views on it.

**It makes the moral frame structural.** "Institutions held to account" stops being a line the
narrator says at the end and becomes the shape of every segment.

**It escalates harder than a body count.** A hundred deaths nobody saw coming is a tragedy.
Three that somebody predicted in writing and filed is something worse.

**It's defensible.** Anyone can pull clips. Pulling inspection histories and inquest findings at
volume takes a research team. You have one; a solo competitor doesn't.

**And it's a better paid layer.** "The document we couldn't put on YouTube" beats "level 8."

Format untouched: seven levels, 35 to 50 cases, 45 to 60 minutes, footage on every segment.

One thing I'm not going to do is apply it to all three channels. Two of them sit inside
mandatory inspection regimes that generate paper by law. The third doesn't, and its problem is
different anyway — so it gets a different answer.

## Pitch 1 · Abyssal Detective — "the Most Disturbing Structural Collapses Iceberg" — 3 min

*[SLIDE: pitch-collapses.png]*

**The document: the engineering report.**

AD's disaster lane has done explosion, roller coaster, mountain, outdoor, water park, Disney
park and incidents at sea. It has never done the thing built by people that fell down while
those people were inside. It's also the most-filmed disaster category there is — security
cameras, dashcams, phone video, construction monitoring — which matters, because thin footage is
exactly what's been sinking AD's recent slate.

**Framework.** Seven levels, **44 cases**, 55 minutes. Levels escalate on scale and on who was
inside. Every segment closes on the paperwork.

- **L1** — cranes, scaffolding, facades. Spectacular footage, small tolls. *6*
- **L2** — bridges. Genoa's Morandi bridge, **43 dead**, on dashcam. FIU, **6 dead**, on a
  traffic camera — and a conference call two days earlier where the cracking was discussed and
  the road stayed open. *6*
- **L3** — buildings that fell mid-construction, onto the people building them. *6*
- **L4** — public venues. Hyatt Regency, **114 dead** — a walkway redesign approved over the
  phone that doubled the load on a connection nobody recalculated. *6*
- **L5** — occupied residential. Surfside, **98 dead**, and a 2018 report describing major
  structural damage to the slab, three years out. *6*
- **L6** — industrial. Rana Plaza, **1,134 dead**, cracks photographed the day before, workers
  ordered back in. *6*
- **L7** — dam and tailings failures, where the toll stops being countable. *8*
- **L8** — Patreon: the reports themselves.

**Hook.** No narration. Forty seconds of a fixed security camera on an ordinary lobby, timestamp
running, nothing happening. Then the frame fills with dust. Cut to black. *"Nineteen months
before this, a structural engineer wrote a report about this building. Somebody read it. Nobody
did anything."*

**Why it beats a standard AD video.** A footage iceberg promises a clip you haven't seen. This
promises the ceiling above your head, and then tells you it was written down. The dread outlives
the video, which is what an iceberg is supposed to do and mostly doesn't.

**Novelty.** No structural-collapse iceberg exists — nearest neighbour is a general "Devastating
Tragedies" iceberg at 432,000, a different video. Under this framework that matters less than it
used to, but it's true.

**Monetization and gray zone.** No gore needed; the footage is dust and distance. Deliberately
spread across a dozen countries so it isn't built on one nation's legal system, and it touches
no religion and no political debate. Cleanest of the three to ship.

## Pitch 2 · Abyssal Detective 2 — "the Most Disturbing Lift & Cable Failures Iceberg" — 3 min

*[SLIDE: pitch-lifts.png]*

**The document: the inspection certificate.**

Second correction, and I'll own it the same way. I originally pitched this as elevators and
escalators only. Then I checked the supply properly: the US sees about **30 elevator and
escalator deaths a year**, and nearly half are maintenance workers in shafts — almost never on
camera. The footage is abundant at the shallow end and thinnest at exactly the levels that have
to carry the payoff. Forty elevator incidents in a row also walks straight into your "segments
too similar to each other" rule.

So I widened it to every machine that lifts people under a mandatory inspection regime:
elevators, escalators, funiculars, cable cars, ski lifts, mine hoists. Same document, far better
footage, visually distinct segment to segment.

**Framework.** Seven levels, **40 cases**, **48 minutes** — inside spec, correcting my earlier
30-to-35-minute version.

- **L1** — malfunctions, nobody hurt. *5*
- **L2** — entrapments. Hours in a sealed box, on camera. *6*
- **L3** — injuries. Rome Metro escalator, 2018, around twenty hurt. *6*
- **L4** — survived the unsurvivable. José Vergara Acevedo, Santiago: **31 floors in 15
  seconds**, into the roof at about 80 km/h. He lived. *6*
- **L5** — single fatalities. Jingzhou, 2015 — she pushed her son clear as the floor panel gave
  way beneath her. Samuel Waisbren, 30: the door-zone restrictor was disabled, the building had
  been **fined $1,300 for exactly that**, and it was still unfixed. JauMarcus McFarland, 18:
  permit expired the previous August. *6*
- **L6** — multiple dead in one failure. *6*
- **L7** — mass casualty. Stresa–Mottarone, 2021: the haulage cable snapped and the emergency
  brake never engaged, because a **clamp had been fitted to hold it open** — the service manager
  admitted this had become routine practice to avoid downtime. **14 dead**, three arrested for
  manslaughter and intentional removal of accident precautions. And Vaal Reefs, where a
  locomotive fell down a mine shaft onto a crowded hoist: **104 men.** *5*
- **L8** — Patreon: the certificates.

**Hook.** *"There is a certificate in the lobby of your building with a date on it. You have
never once looked at it. Every person in this video was killed by a machine that had one too."*

**Why it beats the compilations.** Existing content in this space is clips with no story — a
segment killer in your own bar. All forty here have a name, a cause and a piece of paper.
Mottarone is the thesis in one case: the brake worked, and somebody clamped it shut because
stopping was inconvenient.

**Monetization.** Nothing at L5 to L7 needs to be shown to land — described, not shown, CCTV cut
before impact. Patreon gets the uncut footage and the certificates.

**Territory it opens.** AD2 gets a repeatable line: machines under mandatory inspection that
killed the people they were certified to carry. Lifts, then boilers and pressure vessels, then
cranes. Every one comes with a paper trail by law.

- **L6** — convicted of murder. *6*

- **L8** — Patreon.

**Hook.** A casting-tape clip — someone bright and likeable, saying why they'd be great on the

**Why this and not another named show.** AO's data says named shows beat generic topics 777,000
to 50,000, and this isn't one show. But that gap was never about the name — Marvel and Anime
Based on Real Events failed because there was nothing underneath them. Every case here names a
show and has a court record under it. That's the rule AO wrote, applied literally.

**Monetization.** Crime and conviction, not suicide — a materially easier category to ship
monetized. Victims named with restraint, no crime-scene material, the record does the work.

## CLOSE — 45 sec

The short version.

You're not failing because the format got old. You're failing because the runtime requirement
quietly took over topic selection, and the filter you already wrote down stopped being applied.
Fix the filter, cap at seven, kill the volume numbers — and stop trying to win on topic novelty,
because that game is over. Win on depth instead. Make the level mean the document.

Three things in this changed while I was making it. Volume turned out not to be the cause once I
controlled for time. The health inspection idea died when I checked whether the records still
existed. And the elevator pitch had to widen once I looked at where the footage actually is.

I've left all three in rather than presenting the tidy version, because you wrote that a
retention curve should be able to prove someone wrong. It's easy to agree with that in an
interview. This is me showing you what it looks like when it happens.

Thanks for watching all of it.
