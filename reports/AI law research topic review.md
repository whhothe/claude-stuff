# Measure What AI Law Actually Does

The best replacement topic is an empirical study of how US courts sanction AI-fabricated citations, built from a coded sample of decisions in Damien Charlotin's public AI Hallucination Cases database. It ranks first because three things line up. The live data are large: about **2,225 decisions worldwide, 1,514 of them in the US, by late October 2026**, though those counts come from secondary trackers. The literature has an explicit empirical gap: no law-review study of sanction outcomes or of whether standing orders work turned up. And the answer does not depend on any ruling expected in 2027. The Japan–Korea comparison stalled for a structural reason, not a lack of effort. Both laws are new, Korea's fines sit behind a grace period of at least a year, and with no enforcement record a comparison of the two can only describe texts. The wider 2023–2026 literature shows the same problem at scale. It is overwhelmingly normative and doctrinal, and independent reviews converge on missing evidence: whether AI laws are complied with, enforced or effective. So the papers that establish something new are the ones that measure a law in action, or that test a doctrinal claim against a fresh primary record. On that basis the shortlist below ranks six projects. After the sanctions study come an audit of California SB 53 disclosures, a doctrinal paper on the GDPR one-stop-shop after the Court of Rome annulled the Garante's OpenAI fine, a case study of executive-branch "preemption without a statute," a coded test of whether AI laws really copy the EU AI Act (this one can reuse the student's Korea work), and a copyright paper on licensing markets as fair-use harm. Many supporting facts in the underlying notes were snippet-verified, blog-sourced or unverified because key sites were blocked during research. The report marks them, and a checklist near the end lists what to confirm before citing.

## A comparison without an enforcement record can only describe

The original topic paired two statutes whose main feature, from a research standpoint, is that nothing had happened under them yet. South Korea's AI Basic Act entered into force on **22 January 2026**. Administrative fines are subject to a grace period of at least one year, which may be extended, and the penalties are modest: up to about KRW 30 million (roughly USD 20,000) for failing to label generative-AI output ([Business Day/Reuters](https://www.businessday.co.za/world/asia/2026-01-22-south-korea-launches-landmark-laws-to-regulate-ai/)). Businesses complain that the terms are vague ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/business/tech-science/20260129/unclear-guidance-vague-terms-in-ai-basic-act-leave-businesses-in-limbo)). Put a penalty-free statute next to a statute in its penalty grace period and you have no outcomes to compare, only drafting choices. The paper had no evidence that could prove its author wrong, and that is why it felt descriptive.

The lesson for choosing a new topic is that a paper "establishes something" when three conditions hold. It makes a claim that could turn out false. It has an evidence base that can test the claim. And a recognised gap shows that nobody has yet run the test. Each project ranked below was chosen against those three conditions, plus a fourth practical one: the claim has to survive the court rulings and legislative changes expected in 2027.

## Live developments are outrunning the scholarship in seven arenas

Bibliometric work shows where the academic weight sits. A Web of Science search for AI governance or regulation in October 2024 returned about **6,000 studies**. Of these, **510 (about 7%)** were specifically about EU AI Act governance. The literature took off from 2017 and surged in the 2020s, with keywords clustering around ethics, education and ChatGPT. These figures are snippet-verified, because arxiv.org was blocked during research ([arXiv 2502.03468](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03468)). A separate study of 6,084 AI-ethics articles names algorithmic fairness, privacy and data security, autonomous vehicles and medical AI as core themes. It comes from a lower-tier venue and is indicative only ([Manea et al. 2025](https://eia.feaa.ugal.ro/images/eia/2025_2/Manea_et_al.pdf)). Policy activity has grown faster still: AI mentions in legislative proceedings across 75 countries rose **21.3% in 2024**, to 1,889, and are up more than ninefold since 2016 ([AI Index 2025](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report/policy-and-governance)). No bibliometric source compares AI-law sub-topics with one another. The "heat" ratings below are therefore qualitative, judged from how much scholarship and commentary the researchers found.

The pattern across the notes is a mismatch. In several arenas the facts on the ground changed sharply in 2025–2026, while peer-reviewed scholarship is still digesting 2023–2024 debates. That lag is where a student can add something.

The EU row rests on the Omnibus's legislative record. Parliament adopted it on 16 June 2026, the Council approved it on 29 June, and it was published in the Official Journal on 24 July and entered into force on 27 July 2026 ([DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/2026/The-Digital-AI-Omnibus-Proposed-deferral-of-high-risk-AI-obligations-under-the-AI-Act); [EUR-Lex ELI 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj?locale=en)). The deferral of Annex III duties to December 2027 is corroborated by compliance trackers ([Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/eu-ai-act-high-risk-delay-article-50-transparency-consent/)). The Commission's power to fine GPAI providers took effect on 2 August 2026, yet trackers report **no AI Act fine by the Commission or any national authority** as of late September or early October 2026 ([Vorplabs tracker](https://vorplabs.com/ai-regulatory-updates/eu-enforcement); [aiactbase](https://aiactbase.eu/ai-act-penalties-fines/)). In the meantime the GDPR is the instrument actually being enforced against AI. The Italian Garante's €15m fine on OpenAI was **annulled by the Court of Rome on 18 March 2026** on competence grounds, not on the merits ([Wilson Sonsini](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/openai-prevails-in-landmark-italian-ai-and-gdpr-enforcement-case.html)). In July 2026 the Garante fined Character.AI €158,000 over age verification and minors' protection ([Garante press release](https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/10269594)). The OJ text of the Omnibus was never opened during research, so its exact article wording, including the softened AI-literacy duty, is unverified.

US copyright moved from pleadings to merits. The Third Circuit affirmed in *Thomson Reuters v. Ross* on 29 September 2026. Reportedly it held use of Westlaw headnotes to train a non-generative tool "minimally transformative, at best," and counted harm to a "rapidly developing" market for licensing training data. Every commentator stresses that the ruling **does not decide generative-AI training** ([Ballard Spahr](https://www.ballardspahr.com/insights/alerts-and-articles/2026/10/third-circuit-addresses-fair-use-in-ai-training-but-leaves-generative-ai-questions-unresolved); [IPWatchdog](https://ipwatchdog.com/2026/09/30/third-circuit-affirms-revised-fair-use-ruling-against-ross-ai-legal-research-platform-in-sealed-opinion/)). The Bartz v. Anthropic class settlement of **$1.5 billion**, about $3,100 per work, won final approval on 20 July 2026. Its release covers only past acquisition and copying, not output claims ([Authors Guild](https://authorsguild.org/news/court-grants-final-approval-anthropic-copyright-settlement/)). Cross-motions for summary judgment in the NYT/OpenAI MDL were filed on 4 September 2026 and replies were due on 6 November, with no ruling yet ([OpenAI SJ motion](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/OpenAI-SJ-Mtn-News-Cases-gov.uscourts.nysd_.612697.1496.0.pdf); [ChatGPTiseatingtheworld](https://chatgptiseatingtheworld.com/2026/03/24/summary-judgment-briefing-gets-pushed-back-in-openai-mdl-suit-replies-due-nov-6-instead-of-oct-16/)).

The chatbot-harm field rests on one substantive federal ruling. In *Garcia v. Character Technologies* (M.D. Fla., 21 May 2025), Judge Conway said she was "not prepared" to hold LLM output to be speech at the pleading stage ([Courthouse News](https://courthousenews.com/florida-judge-rules-ai-chatbots-not-protected-by-first-amendment/)). The case then settled in January 2026, so it will never get appellate review (settlement reports are secondary: [AEI via search summary](https://www.aei.org/technology-and-innovation/suicides-settlements-and-unresolved-chatbot-issues-a-long-litigation-road-lies-ahead/)). The ChatGPT cases were coordinated as JCCP No. 5431 in February 2026, with no merits ruling found, and the coordination details come only from blogs and trackers ([AI CERTs News](https://www.aicerts.ai/news/california-coordinates-chatgpt-product-liability-cases/)). Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 in October 2026, and about 40 attorneys general sought child-safety conditions on any sale ([NY AG](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-urges-court-protect-children-and-consumers-characterai)). The action is moving from private tort to public enforcement and bankruptcy, and the scholarship has not followed.

In the US federalism fight, EO 14365 (11 December 2025) directs Commerce to list "onerous" state AI laws and ties BEAD funding to that list ([White House PDF](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/eo-14365.pdf)). The DOJ's AI Litigation Task Force was created on 9 January 2026. As of this research its only court action is the **24 April 2026 intervention in *xAI v. Colorado***, which rests on an Equal Protection theory against duties to prevent disparate impact ([Jenner & Block](https://www.jenner.com/en/news-insights/client-alerts/doj-joins-xai-in-lawsuit-challenging-colorado-ai-act)). Weeks later, on 14 May 2026, Colorado repealed its duty-of-care AI Act and replaced it with SB 26-189, a much narrower transparency law ([Davis Wright Tremaine](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/privacy--security-law-blog/2026/05/colorado-ai-act-repeal-new-transparency-law)). Congress has still enacted no preemption. The Senate voted 99–1 in July 2025 to strip a moratorium from the reconciliation bill ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/news/federal-ai-moratorium-state-preemption-fight-2026)).

## Independent reviews converge on the same missing evidence

The clearest finding across the five note sets is that separate research traditions, using different methods, keep naming the same hole: evidence about whether governance works in practice. A systematic review of AI governance screened 2,918 papers down to 61 studies. **Only 5 of the 61** fully addressed who governs, what is governed, when and how, and governance solutions "lack evidence of effectiveness in practice" ([Batool, Zowghi & Bano, arXiv 2401.10896](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10896v1)). A copy of the paper on Scribd reports 28 articles rather than 61, so check which version you cite. On regulatory sandboxes, one study states that "there has never been a genuine effort to evaluate sandbox effectiveness," even though the Datasphere Initiative counts **66 sandboxes, 31 of them AI-specific, across 44 countries** ([Dardykina, Osuva repository](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/bitstreams/7708e599-06ef-4a25-8ed7-02a2ea0135e7/download); [Datasphere](https://www.thedatasphere.org/datasphere-publish/sandboxes-for-ai/)). The soft-law literature, built on Gutierrez and Marchant's database of 634 programmes, treats unenforceability as the central weakness. Its data stop in 2019 ([OECD.AI Wonk](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/soft-law-2-0)).

### Compliance and enforcement measurement is the most tractable gap

The canonical empirical compliance study is "Null Compliance" (FAccT 2024). Its investigators examined 391 employers subject to New York City's Local Law 144 and found that **only 18 (about 5%) had posted bias audits and 13 (about 3%) transparency notices**. Because employers decide for themselves whether the law covers their tools, non-compliance "cannot be ascertained" ([Wright et al., FAccT 2024](https://facctconference.org/static/papers24/facct24-113.pdf)). The New York State Comptroller's December 2025 audit then found the city's enforcement "ineffective": the city agency reviewed 32 websites and found one instance of non-compliance ([OSC press release](https://www.osc.ny.gov/press/releases/2025/12/dinapoli-new-yorkers-deserve-transparent-hiring-process-when-artificial-intelligence-used-vet-their)). The Comptroller's figures come from search excerpts and law-firm summaries, because the host was blocked. Newer AI-specific statutes show the same pattern. No public enforcement action was found under California SB 53 or Texas TRAIGA ([traiga.news tracker, blog-level](https://traiga.news/traiga-enforcement-what-the-texas-ag-has-and-has-not-done-and-what-comes-next/)), and none under the EU AI Act. Real US AI enforcement has run through older law: FTC Section 5, SEC antifraud rules on "AI washing," and state deception statutes such as the Texas AG's 2024 settlement with Pieces Technologies ([Sidley](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2024/12/rising-ai-enforcement-insights-from-state-attorney-general-settlement-and-us-ftc-sweep)). Even that record is unstable. In December 2025 the FTC set aside its own 2024 Rytr consent order as one that "unduly burden[s] AI innovation" ([FTC order](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Rytr-Order.pdf)). **No authoritative public dataset of AI enforcement actions exists**, and commercial trackers disagree on counts because each defines an action differently ([DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/en/insights/publications/2026/10/three-new-sec-cases-show-continued-scrutiny-of-ai-washing-claims)).

### Deployment-stage, diffusion and Global South gaps are documented but unfilled

An analysis of 1,178 safety and reliability papers from five corporate labs and six universities found that corporate research "increasingly concentrates on pre-deployment areas," such as alignment and testing, while attention to deployment-stage bias "has waned." Only about **15–35% of testing-and-evaluation papers** deal substantively with post-deployment issues. These figures are snippet-verified ([Strauss et al., arXiv 2505.00174](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00174)), and the peer-reviewed version is in *IEEE Access* vol. 14 (2026). MIT's mapping of more than 1,000 governance documents points to "potential gaps" around socioeconomic risks, early lifecycle stages and consumer-facing sectors, again snippet-verified ([MIT AI Risk Initiative](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-april-2026-update)). On diffusion, the most recent journal article on the Brussels effect concludes that a Brussels effect is "neither visible nor… likely" in AI, but concedes a limited evidence base ([Digital Society 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44206-026-00284-0)). The researchers found **no dataset-driven study that codes legislative text** against AI Act provisions. For the Global South, the second Global Index on Responsible AI finds **78% of Global South responsible-AI frameworks non-binding, against 42% in the Global North** ([EU-Africa Chamber summary](https://eu-africa-chamber.org/artificial-intelligence-ai-is-advancing-faster-than-governments-can-protect-people-new-global-index-finds/)). Peer-reviewed doctrinal analysis of African AI-relevant law is scarce. Most work is think-tank commentary or preprints, and one working paper calls the AU Continental Strategy's governance "largely aspirational" ([ResearchGate working paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/396230260_From_Aspiration_to_Implementation_The_African_Union_AI_Continental_Strategy_and_the_Development-Governance_Paradox)).

### Five debates remain open, and new primary records could move them

The open debates are where a doctrinal paper can still take a side, provided it does so with new material. Is LLM output protected speech? Volokh, Lemley and Henderson argue it is protected through creators' and listeners' rights ([JFSL](https://journalspeech.substack.com/p/journal-of-free-speech-law-wheres-62e)). Salib argues it is "no one's" speech ([SSRN 4687558](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4687558)). On whether Section 230 covers generated output, the commentary leans no ([Perault, Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/section-230-wont-protect-chatgpt)), with industry dissent ([Techdirt](https://techdirt.com/2023/03/17/yes-section-230-should-protect-chatgpt-and-others-generative-ai-tools/)). Whether a trained model is itself a "copy" has produced opposite first-instance answers in the UK and Germany. The High Court in *Getty v. Stability* found that the model weights do not store the works, and that point is on appeal ([AIPPI](https://www.aippi.org/news/getty-images-v-stability-ai-uk-high-court-finds-no-secondary-copyright-infringement-in-landmark-genai-ruling/)). The Munich court in *GEMA v. OpenAI* held that memorising lyrics in the weights is reproduction ([Bird & Bird](https://cm.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/landmark-ruling-of-the-munich-regional-court-(gema-v-openai)-on-copyright-and-ai-training)). Samuelson, Nagaraj and Sprigman argue that the fair-use theory of "market dilution" is ill-defined and that whether dilution exists is "an empirical question" no one has answered ([Science 2026](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.ael4888)). In AI federalism, Rubenstein explicitly proposes a "research agenda" for a field he treats as new ([Arizona L. Rev.](https://journals.librarypublishing.arizona.edu/arizlrev/article/10287/galley/9161/download/)). The jurisdictions under-studied relative to their policy activity are Africa and Latin America, including Brazil's PL 2338, still not law in November 2026 ([CASRAI](https://casrai.org/guides/brazil-ai-bill-pl-2338-status)), plus two "negative cases" that diffusion theory rarely uses: Canada, where AIDA died on prorogation in January 2025 ([Lexpert](https://www.lexpert.ca/news/technology-law/ai-laws-update-what-now-after-artificial-intelligence-and-data-act/394712)), and the UK, which has no AI bill.

### 1. What drives sanctions for AI-fabricated citations

**Research question.** Take US decisions from 2023 to 2026 that address AI-fabricated citations or quotations. What predicts whether a court imposes a sanction, and how severe it is? Candidate predictors are filer type (lawyer or pro se), court level and jurisdiction, whether the filer admitted using AI, repeat behaviour, and whether the court had an AI standing order or local rule in force?

**Arguable thesis.** Courts have built a de facto common law of AI-citation sanctions keyed to the existing duty to verify. In that body of decisions, severity tracks filer status and candour, not the presence of an AI-specific rule. If so, the spreading individual standing orders add cost without changing outcomes, which supports uniform local rules over judge-by-judge orders. The data could falsify this: sanctions could turn out harsher where standing orders exist, or pro se litigants could be treated no differently from lawyers. Either result is a finding.

**Gap filled.** Measuring hallucination rates is well covered. Dahl, Magesh, Suzgun and Ho found rates of **58% (GPT-4) to 88% (Llama 2)** on verifiable federal-case questions ([Journal of Legal Analysis](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/large-legal-fictions-profiling-legal-hallucinations-in-large-language-models)), and they present their typology as "a conceptual framework for future research." What the court responses look like is not. The liability researchers found **no law-review empirical study of sanction outcomes or of whether standing orders work**, only trackers and trade commentary. A 2026 preprint asserts that sanctions are "reactive and uneven" and that pro se litigants are disproportionately affected, which is exactly the claim this design would test ([arXiv 2606.21155](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21155); not fetched, and its authorship is unverified). Former Judge Paul Grimm, Maura Grossman and co-authors argue that individual standing orders are unnecessary, confusing and costly ([Bloomberg Law summary](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/employment/judges-reflect-on-genai-use-one-year-after-chatgpts-debut)). That too is a testable claim no one has tested.

**Primary sources and data.** The core dataset is Charlotin's database. Secondary trackers report it at **2,225 decisions as of 27 October 2026 (1,514 US)**. By filer, as of 19 October they report 1,267 pro se, 872 lawyers, 32 judges and 17 experts ([haqq.ai](https://www.haqq.ai/blog/ai-legal-hallucination-audit); [Inside Legal AI](https://www.insidelegalai.com/articles/2026-09-09-legal-ai-hallucination-database-2028-cases)). It was growing by just under eight entries a day in mid-2026 ([UNC Law Library](https://library.law.unc.edu/2026/06/handling-hallucinations-a-reflection/)). The underlying opinions are on CourtListener and court websites. For the standing-order variable, use Bloomberg Law's review, which found at least **14 of 196 federal court websites** with official AI guidance ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/ai-standing-orders-proliferate-as-federal-courts-forge-own-paths)), plus individual judges' pages. A reported Ropes & Gray count of 161 orders is secondary. A sample of 250–300 US decisions, stratified by year and filer type, is enough for cross-tabulations and a simple ordered-outcome model. For a policy hook, use the proposed Federal Rule of Evidence 707 on machine-generated evidence. Its comment period closed in February 2026, and the Advisory Committee reportedly withdrew it as published in May 2026 ([Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/machine-generated-evidence-challenges-the-federal-rules-inside-the-contested-proposed-federal-rule-of-evidence-707); verify on uscourts.gov). A distinctive extension covers judges' own AI errors. In October 2025, Judges Wingate (S.D. Miss.) and Neals (D.N.J.) acknowledged that staff had used AI on orders that were later withdrawn ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/us-law-week/judges-called-out-for-nonfactual-rulings-admit-to-use-of-ai)).

**Scholarship to engage.** Engage Dahl et al. (2024), the Stanford study of commercial legal-research tools (Magesh et al., "Hallucination-Free?", arXiv 2405.20362; its journal venue is unverified), the Grimm and Grossman critique, the Judicature literature, and the "Who Checks the Citations?" preprint. Treat *Mata v. Avianca* (S.D.N.Y. 2023) as the anchor case; its reporter citation was not re-verified in this research.

**Feasibility and risks.** Feasibility is high. Everything is public, nothing depends on a 2027 ruling, and the coding scheme is under the student's control. The risks are methodological, and the paper must state them. First, the database logs decisions that address AI-fabricated content, including some where AI use was alleged but not confirmed. It is not a census of sanctions. Second, it captures only incidents that reached a written decision, so it says nothing about how often the problem goes undetected. Third, standing orders are not randomly assigned: courts that have had problems adopt them. So the paper should claim association, not causation. Fourth, the database website itself was blocked during research, so its fields and export options are unconfirmed, and all counts above are secondary. The biggest risk is being scooped. Before committing, search SSRN and Google Scholar for "hallucination sanctions empirical," "AI citations Rule 11 empirical" and Charlotin's own publications.

### 2. Did SB 53 change frontier-AI disclosure or just codify it?

**Research question.** Do the large frontier developers covered by California's Transparency in Frontier AI Act publish the frameworks and transparency reports it requires? And do those documents differ in substance from the voluntary safety frameworks the same firms published before 1 January 2026?

**Arguable thesis.** SB 53 largely codified existing industry self-regulation rather than changing it. Its marginal effect lies in making disclosure enforceable by the Attorney General and in the incident-reporting channel, not in the content of what firms disclose. That makes it a test case for whether turning soft law into hard law changes behaviour. The thesis is falsifiable: a document comparison could show substantive new commitments.

**Gap filled.** SB 53 has been in force since **1 January 2026**. It covers models trained above **10^26 FLOP**, and developers with revenue over $500 million must publish frontier AI frameworks and transparency reports ([Stanford CodeX gap analysis](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/01/11/californias-transparency-in-frontier-artificial-intelligence-act-gap-analysis/); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/californias-sb-53-understanding-obligations-effect-now-and-2027)). Critical safety incidents must go to the California Office of Emergency Services within 15 days ([Assembly analysis](https://apcp.assembly.ca.gov/media/1019)). The scholarly treatment is a Stanford analysis concluding that the law delivers "transparency and incident reporting without substantive safety mandates," and an essay calling it a "disclosure gambit" ([Stanford "Disclosure Gambit"](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/01/15/californias-disclosure-gambit-what-sb-53-reveals-about-our-relationship-with-potentially-dangerous-technology-2/)). Both are snippet-verified, because the Stanford site was blocked. The researchers found **no peer-reviewed or empirical study of SB 53 or New York's RAISE Act**. The soft-to-hard-law question is open because the soft-law evidence base stops in 2019.

**Primary sources and data.** Start with the statute ([LegiScan bill text](https://legiscan.com/CA/text/SB53/id/3270002)) and the Assembly analysis. The main corpus is the developers' published frameworks and transparency reports, with pre-2026 versions recovered through the Internet Archive. For a scoring rubric, adapt the elements of the "Audit Cards" proposal: its analysis of **24 frontier evaluation reports** found key context routinely missing, including auditor identity, access level and process integrity ([arXiv 2504.13839](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13839v2)). For method, follow the Null Compliance web-audit design ([Wright et al.](https://facctconference.org/static/papers24/facct24-113.pdf)). As a comparator, use New York's RAISE Act. It was signed in December 2025, amended on 27 March 2026 to align with SB 53, and takes effect on 1 January 2027, with penalties up to $1 million for a first violation and $3 million thereafter ([Cooley](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-03-31-new-yorks-frontier-ai-law-gets-a-california-makeover-with-some-key-differences); [FPF comparison](https://fpf.org/blog/the-raise-act-vs-sb-53-a-tale-of-two-frontier-ai-laws/)).

**Scholarship to engage.** Engage the Stanford gap analysis and essay, FPF's comparison, the soft-law literature (Gutierrez and Marchant's "Soft Law 2.0"; the IEEE special issue finding soft and hard law "complementary," via [technologyandsociety.org](https://technologyandsociety.org/?p=5896)), Wright et al. on disclosure laws, and the audit-reporting literature.

**Feasibility and risks.** Feasibility is medium-high, with one critical caveat. That a public corpus of SB 53 disclosures now exists is the researchers' *inference* from the effective date. They did not confirm it by finding the documents. Spend the first day confirming that each in-scope developer has published, and how. Other risks: the number of firms is small, so this is a rubric-based qualitative study, not a statistical one. Cal OES publishes no statistics on incident reports. The penalty ceiling is reported as up to $1 million per violation, but it was cut from $10 million during passage, so check it against the chaptered text. Whether RAISE kept an annual third-party audit requirement is unconfirmed. Federal preemption is a live threat: blog trackers call SB 53 "high preemption risk," though no federal suit has been filed against it ([secureprivacy.ai, blog](https://secureprivacy.ai/blog/trump-ai-executive-order-and-state-law-preemption-what-to-do-now)). Fix a status cutoff date and treat any 2027 RAISE filings as a planned extension.

### 3. The GDPR one-stop-shop as a shield for generative AI

**Research question.** After the Court of Rome annulled the Garante's OpenAI decision because OpenAI had established itself in Ireland before the decision, what power do national data-protection authorities keep over non-EU generative-AI firms? Does the Garante's July 2026 Character.AI decision show where that power now lies?

**Arguable thesis.** The GDPR is functioning as Europe's de facto AI child-safety law, but its reach depends on corporate structure. A firm that sets up an EU main establishment moves supervision to a single lead authority and out of reach of the most active national regulator. The Garante could act against Character.AI partly because that company lacked an EU base: one violation was its late appointment of an EU representative under Article 27. The AI Act's deferred timetable leaves this arbitrage open until at least late 2027.

**Gap filled.** Both decisions are too recent for peer-reviewed analysis. The OpenAI annulment came on 18 March 2026, and the merits questions (legal basis for training, transparency, age verification) were left undecided ([Wilson Sonsini](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/openai-prevails-in-landmark-italian-ai-and-gdpr-enforcement-case.html)). The Character.AI decision (no. 487, 3 July 2026) found violations of Articles 12–14, 24–25, 27 and 35 and ordered fixes to age verification within 120 days ([Garante](https://www.garanteprivacy.it/home/docweb/-/docweb-display/docweb/10269594); [provvedimentigaranteprivacy.it](https://provvedimentigaranteprivacy.it/provvedimenti/character-technologies-10269571/)). The researchers judged a paper on "the one-stop-shop as a shield" both original and buildable entirely from public decisions.

**Primary sources and data.** Use the Garante decisions on OpenAI, Replika/Luka (a €5m fine announced in May 2025) and DeepSeek (a block in January 2025). The Replika and DeepSeek facts come from secondary trackers ([gdprfine.com](https://gdprfine.com/dpa/italy-garante)). Add the Court of Rome judgment, which was not opened during research, EDPB Opinion 28/2024 on AI models and anonymity ([EDPB](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/opinion-board-art-64/opinion-282024-certain-data-protection-aspects_en)), and the GDPR's cooperation and consistency provisions.

**Scholarship to engage.** Engage the 2026 overview of the EDPB opinion ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2212473X26000416)), Velli in *European Papers* ([PDF](https://www.europeanpapers.eu/system/files/pdf_version/EP_EF_2024_H_004_Federica_Velli_00822.pdf)), Wachter on loopholes in the AI Act and liability rules ([YJoLT](https://yjolt.org/node/1112)), and the European Parliament's study on how the AI Act interacts with the GDPR and DSA ([EP study](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/STUD/2025/778575/ECTI_STU(2025)778575_EN.pdf)).

**Feasibility and risks.** Feasibility is medium-high. The decisions are public but largely in Italian, and the full Court of Rome judgment must be obtained. A Cassation appeal is possible and was not checked, and a reversal would reshape the thesis. Write the argument so that it holds whichever way any appeal comes out. Other DPAs' AI actions (Irish DPC, CNIL, Dutch AP) were not researched and should be surveyed as comparators.

### 4. Preemption without a statute: EO 14365 and Colorado

**Research question.** Has the federal campaign against state AI law achieved de facto preemption without a federal statute or a merits ruling? Which legal theory actually carries it?

**Arguable thesis.** Federal preemption of state AI law is working through regulatory chill, not Supremacy Clause displacement. The levers are litigation leverage, funding conditions and the DOJ's Equal Protection attack on disparate-impact duties. Colorado's repeal-and-replace is the proof case. The Equal Protection theory, not the dormant Commerce Clause rationale the order puts up front, is the real threat to state algorithmic-discrimination laws.

**Gap filled.** Commentators across the spectrum agree that an executive order cannot itself preempt state law ([Firewall blog](https://www.thefirewall-blog.com/2026/03/the-curious-case-of-executive-order-14365s-impact-on-ai-regulation/)). Brookings doubts that the dormant Commerce Clause invalidates non-protectionist state AI laws ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/constitutional-constraints-on-regulating-artificial-intelligence/)). Yet the researchers found **no law-review article squarely on EO 14365, the Task Force or *xAI v. Colorado***. The debate lives on Lawfare and in think-tank posts ([Bullock, LawAI](https://law-ai.org/ai-federalism-the-right-way-to-do-preemption/); [Rozenshtein & Ball, Lawfare](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/congress-should-preempt-state-ai-safety-legislation)).

**Primary sources and data.** Use the EO text, the DOJ intervention ([DOJ release, snippet only](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-intervenes-xai-lawsuit-challenging-colorados-algorithmic-discrimination)), the *xAI v. Colorado* docket on CourtListener, Colorado's legislative record for SB 26-189 (to test whether the litigation drove the repeal), and pending federal bills such as H.R. 5388 and H.R. 8037 ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/5388/text)). Under SB 26-189 the duty of care, impact assessments and risk-management programs are gone. What remains is pre-use disclosure, explanation of adverse outcomes and human review "where commercially reasonable," enforced only by the AG after rulemaking ([Eckert Seamans](https://www.eckertseamans.com/legal-updates/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-its-landmark-ai-statute-what-businesses-need-to-know)).

**Scholarship to engage.** Engage Rubenstein's "Federalism & Algorithms," Bullock, Rozenshtein and Ball, the Brookings constitutional analysis, and on the discrimination side Selbst and Barocas on FTC unfairness authority ([Regulatory Review summary](https://www.theregreview.org/2024/01/25/shaikh-how-the-ftc-could-regulate-algorithmic-discrimination/)). *Mobley v. Workday* is the live employment test case. Its 2026 orders let FEHA and ADA claims proceed and held that bias-testing data may be privileged ([Duane Morris](https://blogs.duanemorris.com/classactiondefense/2026/06/24/california-federal-court-grants-in-part-and-denies-in-part-workdays-motion-to-dismiss-in-mobley-v-workday/)).

**Feasibility and risks.** This is the hottest topic on the list and the most exposed to events. Sources conflict on whether *xAI* was stayed on 27 April or by joint motion on 28 August 2026. A single unverified source reports an "EO 14434" (29 September 2026) that folds the Task Force into a new unit ([Lexology](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=39da867c-06d2-49a1-ad4b-e4abb32fb14d)); check the Federal Register before relying on it. Commerce's list of "onerous" laws has apparently not been published, and the FTC's policy statement under Section 7 of the EO was only proposed as of July 2026 ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/a-look-at-the-ftcs-current-approach-to-ai-regulation)). A first standalone DOJ suit or an *xAI* ruling in 2027 could overtake the paper. The causal claim that federal pressure produced the Colorado repeal needs legislative-history evidence, not just the timing.

### 5. Testing the Brussels effect by coding statutory text

**Research question.** Do AI laws and bills enacted or advanced outside the EU since 2024 reproduce the AI Act's provisions? Where they converge, is the AI Act the plausible source, or a common predecessor?

**Arguable thesis.** Convergence with the AI Act is real but narrow. It is concentrated in labelling of synthetic content, where China's rules predate the AI Act. That makes parallel or OECD-channel diffusion a better explanation than a Brussels effect. And because the Omnibus postpones the EU's own high-risk regime, Korea's lighter statute now applies its rules before the "first mover" does.

**Gap filled.** The debate has moved from expecting a Brussels effect ([Siegmann & Anderljung, GovAI](https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/brussels-effect-ai)) to scepticism ([Engler, Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-limited-global-impact-of-the-eu-ai-act); [Digital Society 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44206-026-00284-0)). Almada and Radu argue that whatever spreads may export the Act's weak protection of fundamental rights ([German Law Journal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/032C72AEC537EBB6AE96C0FD90387E3E)). Corporate (de facto) evidence exists: a Thomson Reuters Foundation analysis of 2,973 companies found the effect "visible" but concentrated, reported via [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2026/07/30/how-the-eu-ai-act-is-reshaping-company-rules-from-washington-to-tokyo) from a snippet. The researchers found **no systematic coding of legislative text**. This is also the project that rescues the student's prior work. Their knowledge of Korea's AI Basic Act becomes one data point in a test rather than the whole paper.

**Primary sources and data.** Use the OECD.AI policy database and CSET's AGORA, which holds more than 1,000 laws and policies with full text and tags ([ETO AGORA](https://agora.eto.tech/collections)). Code against: Korea's AI Basic Act and its Enforcement Decree (the decree was not verified); Brazil's PL 2338/2023 as passed by the Senate; China's Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content, effective 1 September 2025 with standard GB 45438-2025 ([Loeb & Loeb](https://loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/chinas-ai-labeling-measures-and-mandatory-national-standards-take-effect-september-1)); India's voluntary guidelines of November 2025 ([S.S. Rana](https://ssrana.in/articles/meity-unveils-india-ai-governance-guidelines/)); Canada's dead AIDA; and the Osborne Clarke redline of the AI Act as amended by the Omnibus ([PDF](https://www.osborneclarke.com/system/files/documents/26/08/11/AI-Act-Redline-Digital-Omnibus-on-AI-%2810-8-2026%29.pdf)).

**Scholarship to engage.** Engage Bradford's *Brussels Effect* and *Digital Empires* (background, not re-verified), Siegmann and Anderljung, Almada and Radu, Engler, Crum on experimentalist governance, the Bologna Law Review piece on Brazil and Chile ([PDF](https://bolognalawreview.unibo.it/article/download/21439/21169/106780)), and a 2026 isomorphism analysis comparing the AI Act with Korea's law ([JAIC](https://aicjournal.org/journal/index.php/jaic/ko/article/download/1/2)). That journal is new and low-profile, but its existence means the Korea leg alone is not untouched.

**Feasibility and risks.** Feasibility is medium. Coding reliability is the main threat: write a codebook, code a subset twice, and report agreement. Textual similarity does not prove copying, so pair the coding with legislative-history evidence of intent wherever it exists. Chinese and Portuguese sources need translation. Brazil could pass its bill in 2027, which is an update rather than a refutation. The Korea grace period and the Brazilian bill status come from secondary sources.

### 6. Licensing markets as fair-use harm after *Ross*

**Research question.** When should a nascent market for licensing AI training data count as cognizable harm under fair-use factor four, and what evidence should prove that such a market exists for the works at issue?

**Arguable thesis.** *Ross*'s recognition of a "rapidly developing" licensing market invites circularity: any use becomes harmful once someone offers to license it. The 2020–2025 deal evidence shows a fragmented market that pays intermediaries, not creators, and whose benefits are fading. Courts should therefore require proof of a functioning market for the specific class of works rather than inferring harm from the fact that deals exist.

**Gap filled.** Samuelson, Nagaraj and Sprigman call market dilution an unproven empirical question ([Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.ael4888)). An economics research agenda finds that **only 7 of 24 major licensing deals compensate original creators** ([arXiv 2510.24990](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.24990)). Brookings, using TollBit data, reports that the click-through "deal premium" publishers enjoyed had essentially vanished by Q4 2025 ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/same-gatekeepers-new-tollbooths-in-the-ai-content-licensing-market/)). Commercial trackers disagree wildly on deal counts (89 against 312), so no authoritative dataset exists ([Emet Research](https://emetresearch.framer.ai/blogs/market-report-ai-data-licensing-deals-(2020-present))).

**Primary sources and data.** Use the *Ross* Third Circuit opinion, *Kadrey v. Meta*, 788 F. Supp. 3d 1028 ([copyright.gov summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/Kadrey-v-Meta-Platforms-Inc-788-F-Supp-3d-1028-ND-Cal-2025.pdf)), the summary-judgment briefs in the NYT/OpenAI MDL, the pre-publication Part 3 of the Copyright Office's AI report (May 2025), the Bartz settlement terms, and the Center for Journalism & Liberty licensing report ([PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5e449c8c3ef68d752f3e70dc/t/69f8a13537aba03b353607d6/1777901877061/Same+Gatekeepers+New+Tollbooths+-+Mapping+the+AI+Content+Licensing+Market+Report+April+2026+WEB.pdf)).

**Scholarship to engage.** Engage Samuelson et al., Sag's "Copyright Safety for Generative AI" ([Houston L. Rev.](https://houstonlawreview.org/article/92126-copyright-safety-for-generative-ai.pdf)), Lemley's "How Generative AI Turns Copyright Upside Down" ([Stanford PDF](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/2024-09-30_How-Gerative-AI-Turns-Copyright-Upside-Down.pdf)), and Cooper and Grimmelmann on memorisation ([arXiv 2404.12590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12590)).

**Feasibility and risks.** The sources are excellent, but the field is the most crowded on this list and the most exposed to 2027. The NYT MDL summary-judgment ruling, the *Getty* Court of Appeal decision, the *GEMA* appeal at the OLG München and the CJEU's *Like Company* ruling (C-250/25) are all expected next year ([European Copyright Society](https://europeancopyrightsociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/ecs-comment-on-the-like-company-reference.pdf)). The *Ross* opinion was reportedly sealed at first, behind a one-line public order. Frame the paper around what evidence *should* count, so that it survives whichever way the courts rule.

## Conclusion

The literature's deepest shortfall is not a missing topic but a missing kind of evidence. Scholars have argued at length about what AI law *should* do. Almost no one has measured what any AI law, court rule or enforcement regime *does*. The 2025–2026 record now makes that measurement possible for the first time: the first decisions under new regimes, a large public database of court incidents, statutory disclosures, and fresh regulator decisions. At the same time, the most-watched questions (generative-AI fair use, chatbot speech, AI Act enforcement, federal preemption) hang on rulings due in 2027. A student paper is therefore safest and most original when it measures behaviour that has already happened, instead of predicting how a pending ruling will come out.

The recommendation is the **empirical study of sanctions for AI-fabricated citations**. Code a stratified sample of about 250–300 US decisions from the Charlotin database and test whether sanction severity tracks filer status and candour rather than the existence of AI standing orders. The study puts a number on a claim that is so far only asserted in commentary and a preprint, uses only public sources, and gives a clear answer whichever way the data fall. Before committing, spend one day on three checks: search SSRN for prior empirical sanctions studies, confirm that the database's fields can be exported, and pull 20 underlying opinions to pilot the codebook. If any check fails, fall back to the SB 53 disclosure audit. Choose it as the primary topic instead if staying close to statutory AI regulation matters more than sample size.
