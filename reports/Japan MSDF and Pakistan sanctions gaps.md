# Pakistan's delayed yen loans offer the cleaner gap

Both candidate topics have publishable gaps, but they differ in kind. **Topic 2 (Japan's 1998 nuclear-test sanctions on Pakistan, the 26 October 2001 lifting, and the August 2005 resumption of new yen loans) has the more defensible and less contested gap.** It also sits more squarely inside "diplomacy", in the sub-field of economic statecraft, meaning sanctions and aid diplomacy in the service of nuclear non-proliferation. No study found in English, Japanese or Pakistani outlets treats Pakistan as a case in its own right. The four-year lag between lifting the sanctions and lending again has only an official explanation, Japanese concern over Pakistan's "debt-bearing capacity", and nobody has tested it ([JICA report, Part III](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737731_05.pdf)). **Topic 1 (the December 2019 decision to send MSDF units to the Middle East independently of the US-led IMSC) is more squarely security and defence policy.** Its gap is also real: no peer-reviewed process-tracing or decision-making study surfaced in English, Japanese or Gulf sources. But that gap is more exposed. The 2026 Iran war and Hormuz crisis are already producing retrospectives on the deployment ([The Diplomat, May 2026](https://thediplomat.com/2026/05/japans-forgotten-deployment-to-the-middle-east/); [The Diplomat, Oct 2026](https://thediplomat.com/2026/10/japan-has-been-here-before-the-1987-persian-gulf-crisis/)), and Japanese-language databases (CiNii, J-STAGE, Diet minutes) could not be searched directly. A student with lived experience in Japan, Pakistan and the Gulf could do either. The Japan–Pakistan combination matches Topic 2's need to join Japanese-side and Pakistani-side sources, which no existing work does. **Recommendation: choose Topic 2 for the thesis. Before committing, read four works in full (Jain 2026, Malik 2008, Hoshiro 2022 and Nikitina and Furuoka 2008) and run a CiNii/Diet-minutes check.** Topic 1 can be kept as a shorter policy or commentary piece where its 2026 relevance pays off. ## The 2019 dispatch is mentioned everywhere but explained nowhere

The December 2019 decision appears in many works but is the main subject of none. The published record splits into four layers:

- broad structural or interpretive scholarship that treats 2019 as one example;
- think-tank commentary, mostly written before the decision or long after it;
- Japanese legislative briefs and legal critiques that describe the legal basis but do not explain the choice;
- Gulf media coverage, which treats the mission as a minor, welcome adjunct rather than something to study.

Across all four, the notes found **no peer-reviewed article, chapter or thesis whose main task is explaining why Tokyo chose an independent mission over joining the IMSC, and none using process tracing**.

### What the decision actually was

The Cabinet decision of 27 December 2019 framed the dispatch as "Japan's independent initiative". Its legal basis was the "research and study" clause, Art. 4(1)(xviii) of the MOD Establishment Act. Its area of operations was the high seas of the Gulf of Oman, the northern Arabian Sea and the Gulf of Aden, and it **explicitly excluded the Strait of Hormuz and the Persian Gulf**. The MOD justified that exclusion on the grounds that Japan-related ships there pass through the territorial waters of coastal states, including Iran and Oman ([MOD Defense of Japan 2020](https://www.mod.go.jp/en/publ/w_paper/wp2020/pdf/R02030106.pdf); [MOD page](https://www.mod.go.jp/en/d_architecture/m_east/index.html)).

The force was one helicopter-carrying destroyer and two P-3Cs, with about 260 personnel on a one-year term. It was the **first MSDF overseas operation since the 2015 security laws** ([Nikkei Asia](https://asia.nikkei.com/Politics/International-relations/Japan-Cabinet-approves-MSDF-units-dispatch-to-Middle-East3); [Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2019/12/27/national/japan-sdf-dispatch-middle-east/)). Weapons use was not permitted unless a maritime security operation was ordered in an emergency ([Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2019/12/27/national/japan-sdf-dispatch-middle-east/)). Suga's formula was that Tokyo "won't join the United States, but will cooperate closely with them" ([Japan Today](https://japantoday.com/category/politics/Japan-to-dispatch-SDF-to-Strait-of-Hormuz-instead-of-joining-U.S.-led-coalition)).

The mission has since been extended every year. Most recently, on 6 November 2026 it was extended to 19 November 2027 with one destroyer and one P-1. These details come from snippets and need confirming ([NHK](https://news.web.nhk/newsweb/na/nd-20261106de71400); [Nippon.com/Jiji](https://www.nippon.com/en/articles/-/57011)). The mission's legal basis did not change through the 2023–24 Red Sea crisis, when Japan stayed out of Combined Maritime Forces escort operations ([SPF IINA](https://www.spf.org/iina/en/articles/nakamura_06.html)).

## The 2001–2005 loan lag has only an official explanation

For Topic 2, the literature is thin in a different way. The episode appears in three kinds of work:

- a case, table row or data point in studies of Japan's ODA Charter and aid sanctions;
- a passage in studies centred on India or South Asian nuclear politics;
- a paragraph in Pakistani descriptive surveys.

Almost every source explains the lifting by **9/11 and Pakistan's role in the anti-terror coalition**, and treats Pakistan together with India. **No stand-alone article on the Pakistan case surfaced in any language, and no scholarly treatment of the gap between the 2001 lifting and the 2005 loans was found.**

### The policy sequence the thesis would explain

In May 1998 Japan froze new grant aid to Pakistan (except emergency, humanitarian and grassroots grants) and froze new yen loans "immediately". It also took a cautious stance on multilateral development bank lending ([MOFA press conference, 29 May 1998](https://www.mofa.go.jp/announce/press/1998/5/529.html); [ODA Summary 1999](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/oda/summary/1999/ref3_05.html)). US congressional reporting records that resumption was conditioned on CTBT signature and non-proliferation assurances ([CRS 98-570](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/19981124_98-570_6ba82961282f5bb2b429282bbb444eeda17df798.pdf)).

On **26 October 2001**, Japan "discontinued" the measures for both India and Pakistan. It praised their contributions to the anti-terror coalition, and officials preferred the word "suspension" so the measures could be reinstated if the test moratoria broke ([MOFA, 26 Oct 2001](https://www.mofa.go.jp/announce/press/2001/10/1026.html); [CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2001/WORLD/asiapcf/east/10/26/japan.sanctions/index.html)). CNN called the move "controversial in Japan" ([CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2001/WORLD/asiapcf/east/10/26/japan.sanctions/index.html)). The 2002 ODA White Paper adds two official reasons, progress on non-proliferation and the need for mid- to long-term support for Pakistan ([ODA白書2002](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/oda/shiryo/hakusyo/02_hakusho/ODA2002/html/honpen/hp2030200.htm)). After the lifting, Japan announced **US$300m in grant aid** ([ODA White Paper 2002](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/oda/white/2002/part1_2_2_3.html)).

**New yen loans stayed frozen.** A JICA report says they remained suspended because of concern over Pakistan's debt-bearing capacity (債務負担能力への懸念). Post-2001 lending was limited to continuing phases of the Kohat Tunnel project, about ¥4.0bn in FY2001 and about ¥3.1bn in 2002 ([JICA Part III](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737731_05.pdf); [Part I](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737731_02.pdf)). The exact title and year of that JICA report were not captured.

The resumption then came in steps:

- **August 2004:** the government judged that Japan should support Pakistan's stable development to correct the poverty that breeds terrorism ([MOFA ODA databook 2005](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/oda/shiryo/kuni/05_databook/pdfs/02-04.pdf)).
- **January 2005:** Dawn reported agreement to resume "$500 million annual assistance" ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/401088)).
- **February 2005:** a new Country Assistance Program for Pakistan followed ([MOFA](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/oda/assistance/pdfs/e_pakistan2005.pdf)).
- **30 April 2005:** Koizumi's Islamabad visit signalled resumption, citing Pakistan's "vital role" against terrorism. Al Jazeera put the scale at about US$160m and reports that Koizumi also raised the A.Q. Khan network ([Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2005/5/1/japan-premier-wraps-up-pakistan-visit)).
- **10 August 2005:** JBIC signed two loans totalling **¥16,362m**, for rehabilitating the Lower Chenab Canal system and upgrading a load-dispatch system ([JBIC release](https://www2.jica.go.jp/yen_loan/pdf/en/5435/20050810.pdf)).

These monetary figures ($500m a year, about $160m, ¥16.4bn) measure different things and must be reconciled before any of them is quoted.

## Topic 2 is diplomacy; Topic 1 is security policy that also uses diplomacy

The user asked directly which topic is "diplomacy". The answer is **Topic 2**, in the sub-field of **economic statecraft, specifically aid-sanctions diplomacy within nuclear non-proliferation diplomacy**. Its secondary sub-fields are alliance management (lifting the sanctions alongside the post-9/11 US realignment) and, loosely, coercive diplomacy, because a CTBT condition attached to a punishment fits the demand-plus-threat pattern ([CRS 98-570](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/19981124_98-570_6ba82961282f5bb2b429282bbb444eeda17df798.pdf)). The sequence of imposition, a lifting deliberately worded as a reversible "suspension", and inducement through loans tied to counter-terrorism partnership is negotiated signalling between states ([MOFA, 26 Oct 2001](https://www.mofa.go.jp/announce/press/2001/10/1026.html); [JBIC](https://www2.jica.go.jp/yen_loan/pdf/en/5435/20050810.pdf)).

One nuance to raise in the thesis: in David Baldwin's *Economic Statecraft* (Princeton, 1985), economic statecraft is a technique **separate from** diplomacy in the narrow sense. Broader diplomacy-studies usage treats sanctions and aid as diplomatic instruments. Both definitions were drawn from background knowledge and need page-level verification.

**Topic 1 is more squarely security and defence policy.** Its core act was a Cabinet decision to deploy warships and patrol aircraft under a domestic legal clause, with constitutional and Article 9 questions at stake. Defenders and critics alike argued about it in the vocabulary of SDF law ([JFBA](https://www.nichibenren.or.jp/en/document/statements/191227.html); [JIIA](https://www.jiia.or.jp/eng/report/2021/04/ajiss_commentary/jmsdf-activities-in-the-indo-pacific-region.html)).

Topic 1 can be framed as diplomacy, but only by putting the diplomatic choreography in the foreground:

- **Abe's June 2019 Tehran visit**, the first by a Japanese prime minister in 41 years, carried a Trump message to Khamenei, who declined to engage ([The Diplomat](https://thediplomat.com/2019/06/what-did-japanese-prime-minister-shinzo-abe-accomplish-in-iran/); [Malay Mail/AFP](https://www.malaymail.com/news/world/2019/06/12/japans-abe-in-tehran-mission-to-ease-iran-us-tensions/1761437)).
- **Rouhani's December 2019 Tokyo visit** was the first by an Iranian president in 19 years, and Abe briefed him on the dispatch there ([Diplomatic Bluebook 2020](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/other/bluebook/2020/html/chapter2/c020602.html)).
- **The "not joining but cooperating" message** was aimed at Washington.

In those terms its sub-fields are **alliance management and hedging, summit and mediation diplomacy, and what Christian Le Mière's typology calls cooperative or persuasive maritime diplomacy** ([Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/Maritime-Diplomacy-in-the-21st-Century-Drivers-and-Challenges/LeMiere/p/book/9780415828000); [E-IR review](https://e-ir.info/?p=52323)). Two other labels fit poorly:

- **Gunboat diplomacy.** The mission was explicitly non-coercive and stayed out of Hormuz.
- **Defence diplomacy in Cottey and Forster's sense.** That means the peacetime *cooperative* use of armed forces with partners, and the 2019 deployment was deliberately unilateral ([Durham record](https://dro.dur.ac.uk/1967/)).

The poor fit can be turned into a contribution. "**Unilateral naval presence as two-audience diplomatic signalling**" is a concept the defence-diplomacy literature does not cover well. If the student's programme requires a thesis filed under "diplomacy", however, Topic 1 carries a real risk that a reviewer or examiner reclassifies it as security studies.

## What each thesis would add after publication, and how to test it

### Topic 1: a reconstruction from June to December 2019, tested against five rival explanations

**Is there a gap?** Yes, provisionally. The case for it: no English or Japanese peer-reviewed study explains the choice; the closest works are descriptive (Heng, the 立法と調査 brief, Nishida) or structural (Hughes). What makes it provisional: CiNii, J-STAGE and NIDS publications are unchecked, and 2026 retrospectives are appearing quickly.

**What the paper would add:**

- **Empirical.** The first reconstruction of the decision window. It also adds two variables nobody has examined. The first is the **shipping industry**: the Japanese Shipowners' Association and NYK, after the attack on the Japanese-operated *Kokuka Courageous*. No source treats this as an explanatory variable. The second is **the choice of legal instrument**: why the research clause rather than a maritime security operation or the Anti-Piracy Act. The paper would also bring in Gulf host-state responses.
- **Methodological.** Formal process-tracing tests applied to an SDF dispatch decision. Pohlkamp's dissertation shows the method itself is not new to Japanese security cases, so the novelty is the application, not the technique.
- **Conceptual.** Unilateral, legally minimal naval presence as a hedging and signalling instrument with two audiences.
- **Positional.** It would adjudicate between Miyagi's dual dependence, Hughes's alliance integration and Nakamura and Wright's interdependence. It would test Shinoda's Kantei-leadership and Midford's public-opinion hypotheses in a case where **no new legislation and no Diet vote** removed the legislative veto point that shaped the 2001 and 2003 cases.

**Main decision sequence.** These steps are reconstructable from public sources:

- **13 June 2019:** tanker attacks during Abe's Tehran visit.
- **July 2019:** the US calls for a coalition, and Jiji reports the legal options Japan weighed ([Jiji](https://www.jiji.com/jc/graphics?p=ve_pol_seisaku-anpoboei20190713j-03-w460)).
- **3 September 2019:** reports that Japan will not join ([Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2019/09/03/national/politics-diplomacy/japan-wont-join-us-mideast-gulf-coalition/)).
- **18 October 2019:** Abe instructs the NSC to study an independent dispatch to the Gulf of Oman area ([Nikkei](https://www.nikkei.com/article/DGXMZO51239050R21C19A0PP8000/); [Military Times](https://www.militarytimes.com/flashpoints/2019/10/19/japan-to-send-own-force-wont-join-us-coalition-for-mideast/)).

### Topic 2: a process trace from imposition to delayed relief, with India as the paired comparison

**Is there a gap?** Yes, and it is strong. Four distinct openings exist:

- **Gap 1: explaining the lifting with evidence.** At present the account is assertion: "US pressure" or "war on terror".
- **Gap 2: the 2001–2005 loan lag.** This is the strongest gap and appears unoccupied. Its only explanation is official and untested.
- **Gap 3: Pakistan as its own case rather than an appendix to India.** Pakistan differs on several counts: its higher dependence on Japanese aid, the extra coup-and-democracy layer coded in PIIE case 99-3, and its debt crisis.
- **Gap 4: Japan as an independent sanctions sender.** Standard datasets do not treat it as one.

**What the paper would add:**

- **Empirical.** The first reconstruction, from Japanese and Pakistani sources, of the decision to lift and of the separate decision to lend again. It would also correct a dating error in Pakistani commentary.
- **Methodological.** Explicit process tracing on a Japanese aid-sanction case, structured in phases after Raynor. It would include a **paired comparison with India**: the same measures and the same lifting date, but an earlier thaw and some continued project lending. The India loan timeline needs verification.
- **Conceptual.** The distinction between **formally ending sanctions and actually restoring economic relief** ([POMEPS](https://pomeps.org/the-end-of-sanctions-rethinking-termination-processes-through-the-syrian-case)), applied instrument by instrument to aid sanctions. Grants resumed in 2001 and loans in 2005, which lets a researcher separate MOFA's political logic for grants from the creditor logic of MOF and JBIC for loans.
- **Positional.** It would test Hoshiro's large-N finding that Charter norms do not predict aid decisions at the level of a single case. It would extend Katada's China framework to South Asia. It would challenge DiFilippo's US-pressure story, correct the PIIE framing, and position itself explicitly against Jain (2026) and Malik (2008).

### Risks that could close or narrow each gap

**Topic 1 risks.** The first is **unchecked Japanese-language scholarship**: Japanese journals, NIDS annuals and law reviews may already hold detailed decision accounts. The second is **the 2026 Hormuz crisis**. It has already produced the Tatsumi piece in May, a Diplomat piece in October comparing 2026 with the 1987 Persian Gulf crisis, and Sahashi's Carnegie analysis, and it will draw more retrospective writing on 2019 in both languages during 2026–27. A competing journal article could appear while the thesis is being written. The third is **evidence**: insider sources on a decision taken in 2019 are scarce, and the full texts of Heng (2022) and Hughes (2022) were not read. The mission is also still running, so the outcome being explained keeps moving.

**Topic 2 risks.** First, **four works were not read in full**: Jain DP46 (February 2026), Malik (2008), Hoshiro (2022) and Nikitina and Furuoka (2008). If any of them explains the loan lag in depth, Gap 2 narrows to a methodological contribution. Second, **unpublished Japanese theses** in IRDB were not searched. Third, the **internal deliberations of MOF and JBIC** may not be public, and evaluation reports carry the official line. Fourth, the **conflicting money figures** need reconciling. Fifth, **lower topical salience** makes it a harder pitch to policy-oriented journals, though an easier one for diplomatic-history journals.

## Conclusion: choose Topic 2, verify four readings first, and keep Topic 1 as a commentary piece

**Recommendation: Topic 2.** It fits the "diplomacy" category without needing to be reframed. Its gap is unusually precise and testable: why grants resumed in 2001 but loans waited until 2005. The official record states the grant/loan split and its debt rationale explicitly, so hypotheses can be tested on public evidence. India offers a built-in comparison. The research is not racing a news cycle. And the student's Japan–Pakistan experience is the specific asset that the existing literature, split by language, lacks.

Topic 1 is the better choice only if the programme wants security policy and the student values policy salience over a secure gap. Even then, it needs a fast, thorough Japanese database check, because events in 2026 are working to close its gap. The 2026 relevance of Topic 1 is better used in a short piece, such as the Hague Diplomacy Blog, The Diplomat or the Tokyo Review, built on the five-hypothesis table above, while the thesis develops Topic 2.
