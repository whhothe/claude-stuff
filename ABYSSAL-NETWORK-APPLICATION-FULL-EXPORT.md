## The short version

**Q1.** The network isn't failing because the format aged. It's failing because the
**35–50 case, 45–60 minute requirement quietly took over topic selection**. To fill fifty filmed
segments you need a topic broad enough that nobody has pre-existing morbid curiosity about it —
which is how a slate that once ran *CCTV Incidents* at 3.17M ended up running *Fishing
Encounters* at 65K. Two comfortable explanations were tested and discarded: titles (a constant
through the peak) and volume (confounded with time).

**Q2.** Topic-level novelty is unwinnable — Kitchen Nightmares, child stars, India and Telegram
were all tested and all already carry multi-million-view videos. So rather than three new topics,
one change applied where it fits: **every segment ends on the document.** The inspection that was
failed, the report that was filed, the background check that was run and ignored. Levels still
escalate on harm; the paperwork is the signature of every case.

**Four conclusions in this work were reversed after being written down.** All four are spoken

Build the level ladder before a script exists: how many levels, what defines each one. Ordering is about escalation, not about which entry is most graphic. Escalation in our structure means that every single segment needs to be objectively more disturbing than the one before it.

When a test fails, diagnose it with them. You own the editorial half of that answer, they own the execution half.

Work with senior writers, designers, editors, etc. throughout the whole production cycle. Coordinate with the producer, who is responsible for the logistics. You are in charge of the creative vision.

## Core Premise of the Abyssal Network

*Source: linked from the JD as a Google Doc; supplied by the applicant because this
environment's network proxy blocks docs.google.com. Reproduced as provided.*

### 1.1 The Abyssal promise (this cannot change)

Abyssal tells real life horror stories so the audience never has to go and find them. We do the
research and the deep dive into the horrifying material; we absorb the horror, and the viewer
gets their morbid curiosity closed just by sitting and watching. Five things are true of every
Abyssal video, whatever its format: the material is real and verified; it is disturbing but
survivable (the guide takes the hit); it feels forbidden or obscure, something the viewer could
not or should not find alone; it carries a moral frame (victims honored, perpetrators judged,
institutions held to account); and it is delivered by a human guide. Formats change. Those five
do not.

### 1.2 The current format (what every Abyssal channel runs today)

Every video on all three channels is an iceberg. Seven levels, escalating from Level 1 at the
top to Level 7 at the bottom, each level more disturbing than the one before. Each level holds
roughly 5 to 7 cases (a case is one incident, one story, one event, told in about 60 to 120
seconds over real footage), so a full video carries anywhere from 35 to 50 cases. Videos run 45
to 60 minutes, are narrated by a host over footage, and are chaptered in the description as
Intro, Level 1 through Level 7 (Abyssal Detective adds a Patreon-exclusive Level 8). Titles
follow one formula, "the Most Disturbing X Iceberg," with "[Vol. N]" sequels of topics that
performed. Each channel uploads about twice a week.

That is the format we run today, and it is also our problem: it is aging. The strategist we hire
has to do two things: keep this format running at our standard, and research the format that
comes next. This test measures both.

### 1.3 The channels (what each one publishes right now)

Study the channels themselves on YouTube; the descriptions below are a map, not a substitute.

**Abyssal Observer (AO)** — Television, film, and documentary as the entry point, with documented
real-world harm as the story. Current lanes: TV accidents (Vol. 1-3), talent show accidents
(Vol. 1-3), survivor show accidents, sports TV accidents and deaths, magic and circus TV
accidents, live TV and news gone wrong, banned movies (Vol. 1-3), banned TV episodes, banned
documentaries, banned commercials, reality shows (My 600-lb Life, 90 Day Fiancé, My Strange
Addiction, Cheapskates, Jerry Springer, Live PD), documentaries (asylum, cult, undercover,
Netflix), TV networks, canceled shows, movie accidents (Vol. 4), movies that caused real
tragedies, WWE tragedies, plastic surgery TV. **If you remove the real-life consequence and only
the show is left, it is not an AO topic.**

**Abyssal Detective (AD)** — Raw real-world horror with no entertainment property needed for the
hook: internet content (deleted Reddit threads, Facebook livestreams, TikTok Live, online
marketplaces, things found on the internet, analog horrors, internet users), geography and places
(Chinese, Korean, and Japanese incidents, Appalachian Mountains, videos from each state, Europe,
abandoned towns and buildings, forbidden places, haunted houses), footage lanes (dashcam moments,
drone footage, doorbell footage, courtroom moments, stalkers caught on camera, historical
footage), disasters (explosion, roller coaster, mountain, outdoor, water park, Disney park,
incidents at sea), and disturbing books, warning signs, audios with backstories, fast food and
retail incidents. AD videos end with a Patreon-exclusive Level 8. Movies and shows in any form
belong to AO. True crime with no footage anchor is rejected network-wide.

**Abyssal Detective 2 (AD2)** — Footage-format-led icebergs, where the type of footage or the
setting it was captured in is the hook: CCTV incidents (Vol. 3), dashcam footage (Vol. 5),
doorbell footage (Vol. 4), POV and last-moment footage, security camera footage, incidents caught
on camera (Vol. 3), videos before disaster (Vol. 7), encounters caught on camera (Vol. 3), theme
park incidents (Vol. 5), live TV moments (Vol. 5), and setting-based footage series (trains,
factories, workplaces, roads, fishing, planes, animal attacks, storms, festivals, home invasions,
police traffic). It also carries the network's non-English internet lane (Asian Internet Vol.
1-3, Russian Internet, Japanese Footage, Hispanic TV moments). AD2 uses "[Vol. N]" sequels more
than any other channel in the network.

**Reality requirement, network-wide:** no fiction, no creepypasta, no ARGs, no unverifiable
claims. True crime with no footage or recording anchor is rejected network-wide.

### 1.4 The gray zone

We do not center videos on politically charged subjects, religion, or divisive social debates,
and we do not build videos on a single religion's or nation's legal system. Every proposal must
be YouTube-TOS compliant. Our format runs close to the line and there is a delicate gray zone we
operate inside; that judgment is part of the job once hired.

### 1.5 The segment quality bar

Every segment (one incident, one story, one case) needs 30 or more seconds of usable footage or
recording, a clear 60 to 120 second story structure, a genuinely disturbing element, and
evergreen relevance. Segment killers: photos only, brief viral clips with no story, dead trends,
segments too similar to each other. A topic for the current format must be able to supply 35 to
50 segments that clear this bar (see 1.2); a topic for a new format must be able to fill that
format's runtime without padding.

## Decision Log

Every conclusion that was reached, tested, and then reversed — in order. This exists because
four separate things in this work turned out to be wrong, and the corrections are more useful
than the conclusions.

---

### Reversal 1 — Volume was never the cause

**What was claimed:** the network is over-producing and cannibalising itself. *"Every time
uploads increased, per-video views decreased."* Recommendation: cut from ~20 uploads/month to 12.

**What killed it:** recomputing across all 61 channel-months and controlling for time.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Pooled correlation, cadence vs per-video views, all time | r = **−0.21** |
| Pooled correlation, **2026 only** | r = **−0.13** |
| Abyssal Observer, 2026 only | r = **+0.05** — *positive* |
| 2026 per-video views, ≤5 uploads/mo vs ≥7/mo | **284,952 vs 282,666** — identical |

The apparent effect was almost entirely confounded with time: early months were both low-cadence
and high-view. The specific figures quoted were also cherry-picked single months, not averages —
"4/month → over 1 million" against a real 4-upload-month average of **416,430**.

**Worse, the recommendation was backwards.** In 2026, months with ≥7 uploads earned **2.05M**
total views against **1.22M** for ≤5. Cutting output would have cut revenue.

**What survived:** breaking 2026 out by exact upload count shows **8 is the only tier where total
monthly views collapse** — 7 uploads earns 3.01M, 8 earns 1.09M. And the premise doc says each
channel uploads "about twice a week," which is eight. Their stated cadence is precisely the
breaking tier. The recommendation became *"cap at seven, never eight"* — smaller, sharper, and
actually true.

---

### Reversal 2 — Title monoculture cannot be the cause of a decline

**What was claimed:** 98% of titles use one formula, and this is *"the single most damaging
pattern."*

**What killed it:** the peak videos use the same formula. *Footage Caught on Camera* — 4.03M.
*CCTV Incidents* — 3.17M. Every one of the top thirteen is "the Most Disturbing [X] Iceberg." The
channel reached two million average views **with** 98% identical titles.

A constant cannot explain a change. Titles were demoted from cause to ceiling — still the
highest-leverage untested lever, and the one the JD explicitly gives the strategist, but not the
reason for the fall.

**What replaced it as the diagnosis:** the premise doc's own line — *"the viewer gets their
morbid curiosity closed just by sitting and watching."* A topic only works if there is
pre-existing curiosity to close. Early topics had it (CCTV, Footage Caught on Camera, Internet
Mysteries). Recent ones don't (Biblical Horrors 60K, Amazon Incidents 71K, Fishing Encounters
65K, Chinese Shows 54K). And the cause of *that* is the 35–50-case requirement: to fill fifty
filmed segments you need a topic broad enough that nobody is curious about it.

---

### Reversal 3 — "Zero results on YouTube" was false

**What was claimed:** all three pitch topics verified as novel across YouTube.

**What killed it:** re-running the searches properly.

| Topic | Claimed | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Elevator & escalator | zero | ✅ held |
| Kitchen Nightmares | zero | ❌ three icebergs exist; ground held by Bartolozzi **3.93M** (3 months prior) and MermaidGrove **3.69M** |
| Hotels | zero | ❌ adjacent *Lost Locations Iceberg*, 1.28M |

Replacement candidates died the same way: **India** (icebergs at 307K and 175K), **Telegram**
(145K/188K Spanish, 646K Russian), **child stars** (Deep Dive 4.64M, Nick Crowley 4.41M, j aubrey
5.08M).

**The conclusion that matters:** topic-level novelty is effectively unwinnable. Almost every
subject with real morbid curiosity attached already carries a multi-million-view video. And Q2
asks how your version *"beats the original"* — which presupposes an original exists. Zero-results
was the wrong bar all along.

---

### Reversal 4 — The document axis, in its first form, fails arithmetic

**What was claimed:** make the *level* mean the document. L1 nobody knew → L7 it was in writing
and they signed it off anyway.

**What killed it:** the spec requires **5–7 cases at every tier, including the deepest**. "They
knew and did it anyway" is the rarest state there is. One Mottarone exists; six do not.

**Corrected form:** levels escalate on **harm**, which is abundant at every tier. The document
becomes the **signature of every segment** — every case ends on the paperwork — rather than the
definition of the level. Same obscurity benefit, same moral frame, and it fills.

**Same arithmetic killed Kitchen Nightmares a second time.** One show cannot fill seven levels;
it has one Cerniglia, not six.

### Structural corrections from reading the brief

The JD was never in the repo — earlier work ran on a paraphrase of it. Reading the real JD, and
then the Core Premise doc, corrected several things including some of the corrections.

- **AD2 does not lack an identity.** The premise doc gives it one: footage-format and setting led.
  And the data agrees — its recent half is **55% on-territory** against 28% early, with `Vol. N`
  numbering down from **80% to 25%**. It is mid-repositioning and executing it. The original
  "identity-less sequel dump" diagnosis contradicted both the brief and the data.
- **Disasters belong to AD, not AD2** — which meant the Hotels pitch was not mis-routed for the
  reason first given. It died instead on the footage-anchor rule: *"true crime with no footage
  anchor is rejected network-wide,"* and Hotels was mostly court records and stills.
- **"8 videos per month across three channels" means 8 per channel** — the premise doc says each
  channel uploads twice a week. This reversed an earlier finding that the "cap at 7"
  recommendation was 3× too high; it is in fact one notch below their stated cadence.
- **Two sub-questions were unanswered entirely:** what they are *"protecting for the wrong
  reasons,"* and *"what makes it an Abyssal video."* Both now answered.
- **Three named success metrics had no coverage:** Patreon conversion, monetization and
  advertiser-friendly handling, and retention at level transitions.
- **Q1 opened politely** despite the brief saying *"do not be polite. We are not looking for a
  fan."* Rewritten to open on the worst number.

### Final verification pass

Two assumptions were flagged as unverified on the near-final draft and then researched. **One
failed.**

- ❌ **Kitchen Nightmares health inspection files.** Restaurant inspection retention is typically
  **three years**; the series ran 2007–2014. The records are gone, and no secondary source
  systematically reports pre-filming scores. Fails the premise doc's own gate — *"every segment
  traces to a real source."*
- ⚠️ **Elevator footage supply is inverted.** The US sees ~30 elevator and escalator deaths a
  year, and **nearly half are maintenance workers in shafts** — almost never filmed. Footage is
  thickest at the shallow end and thinnest where the payoff lives. Topic widened to every machine
  under mandatory periodic inspection: elevators, escalators, funiculars, cable cars, ski lifts,
  mine hoists.
- ✅ **New anchor verified:** Stresa–Mottarone, 23 May 2021. Haulage cable snapped; the emergency
  brake never engaged because **a clamp had been fitted to hold it open**. The service manager
  admitted this had become routine practice to avoid downtime. 14 dead. Three arrested for
  manslaughter and intentional removal of accident precautions.

### One caution carried forward

**Do not cite Jamal Trulove.** He appears in coverage as a reality-TV contestant convicted of
murder, which would fit the AO pitch. That conviction was reportedly overturned on appeal and he
was acquitted at retrial. Left out of the pitch deliberately, and flagged here so it is not
picked up later from the same search results.

at **139,000** — **minus 93 percent**. Observer: **1.39 million** to **141,000**, **minus 90**.
Detective 2: **601,000** to **133,000**, **minus 78**.

Each of those peaks was a month with one huge video, so the fairer comparison is 2025 as a whole.
Against that, the last three months are down **82, 67 and 55 percent**. Either way, it happened
inside eighteen months with the format unchanged.

#### What it is not

*[SLIDE: what-it-is-not.png]*

**It's not the titles.** 98 percent of videos use the same formula — including the peaks: *Footage
Caught on Camera* at 4.03 million, *CCTV Incidents* at 3.17 million. A constant can't explain a
change. Titles cap the upside; they didn't cause the fall.

**It's not volume.** Controlling for channel and month, upload count made no measurable difference
to per-video views — every tier sits within about seven percent of what you'd expect. And busier
months brought in more total views, not fewer, so cutting output would lose reach.

**And it's not quality.** On AD, like-to-view went from **1.86 percent** in 2024 to **2.91 percent**
this June. The people who still click like it more than ever. It's a reach problem.

#### What it actually is

*[SLIDE: curiosity-to-close.png]*

The premise doc says the viewer *"gets their morbid curiosity closed just by sitting and
watching."* That's the key: **a topic only works if there's morbid curiosity already there to
close.**

The biggest videos had it built in — *Footage Caught on Camera*, **4.03 million**. *CCTV*, **3.17
million**. *Internet Mysteries*, **2.2 million**. The recent ones don't — *Amazon Incidents*,
71,000. *Fishing Encounters*, 65,000. *Biblical Horrors*, 60,000. *Chinese Shows*, 54,000. Nobody
walks around with unresolved morbid curiosity about fishing.

*[SLIDE: three-tests.png]*

Running those four through the three greenlight tests, I found all four fail on depth and on the
can't-find-it-alone test. *Biblical Horrors* also sits on the religion line in the gray-zone rule.

The filter exists. It isn't being applied.

#### What's being protected for the wrong reasons

*[SLIDE: the-35-case-trap.png]*

**The 45-to-60-minute, 35-to-50-case requirement is creating the topic problem.** At 35 to 50
cases, each needing thirty seconds of footage, a topic has to yield about fifty filmed incidents. Almost nothing with real
morbid curiosity attached is that broad. So greenlight quietly shifts from "what do people want to
know" to "what can we find fifty clips of" — and *Fishing Encounters* is the answer to the second
question.

The iceberg is worth protecting. **The runtime is what's being protected for the wrong reasons.**

#### Four more findings

*[SLIDE: series-check.png]*

**Series.** Of the thirteen multi-volume series where I have the first and the latest volume, nine
are down **88 to 98 percent** — CCTV, Dashcam, Theme Park, Live TV, Talent Show — and they're still
getting new volumes. Two grew: *Cheapskates'* second volume beat its first by **18 percent**, and
*Things on Asian Internet's* by **94**. So the call on which series earn a next volume can be made
from the data. Right now it isn't being made.

*[SLIDE: convergence.png]*

**Convergence.** A fourfold spread in subscribers, and all three channels now sit between **133 and
141 thousand** views. Three audiences on one floor points to one shared cause.

*[SLIDE: the-ask.png]*

**The ask.** Together the channels ask for about **seventeen hours** of viewing a month. The
channels in this niche averaging 2.6 to 3.2 million views a video — Chilling Scares, Nexpo,
Nick Crowley — each ask for **an hour or less**. Upload count isn't what's costing per-video views,
so the lever left is runtime: the million-view videos in this niche cluster around **28 minutes**,
and AD averages **59**. Sixty percent of a 35-minute video is the same 21 minutes as thirty-five
percent of a 60-minute one — but the shorter video shows the better retention.

**Titles.** AD's one non-iceberg title — *4chan Threads That Triggered FBI Investigations* — did
**407,000**, 61 percent above AD's 2026 average. One data point, but titles are the strategist's
lever, and it's the cheapest one there is to test.

So what's fatiguing isn't the iceberg. It's the topics, the repeats, and the size of the ask.

### Pass two: each channel

*[SLIDE: ao-rule.png]*

#### Abyssal Observer

Three of AO's four weakest videos this year are show-first topics that break its own rule —
*Marvel*, 39,545. *Anime Based on Real Events*, 45,563. *Chinese Shows*, 54,395 — and *Directors
Behind Famous Films* did 60,218. Those four average **49,930**.

Its consequence-led named shows — TLC, To Catch a Predator, Intervention, My Strange Addiction, 90
Day Fiancé, Extreme Cheapskates — average **777,580**. And it holds across the whole channel: TV and
reality topics average **483,000** a video, animation and franchise topics **210,000**.

AO isn't exhausted — as recently as June, *Cheapskates'* second volume did **590,000**. Its baseline
is falling because show-first topics are getting through greenlight, and the fix is a rule it
already has.

*[SLIDE: per-channel.png]*

#### Abyssal Detective

The steepest fall and the broadest territory — and the two are linked. With almost anything
technically in-lane, *Biblical Horrors*, 60,277, and *Amazon Incidents*, 70,529, had no grounds to be
refused. It's also the longest channel — **59 minutes** against a **28-minute** sweet spot — so it
asks the most of a viewer while offering the thinnest topics. Its baseline is falling because its
topics got thinner, and a territory that wide gives greenlight no grounds to say no.

*[SLIDE: ad2-shift.png]*

#### Abyssal Detective 2

AD2 has repositioned: the recent half of its catalogue is **55 percent** disaster and accident
topics, up from **28**, and volume numbering is down from **80 percent to 25**. The problem is what
it's being fed, and both supply lines are exhausted.

One is later volumes of AD1's series — Theme Park and Live TV Moments each did about **two million**
on AD1; their latest volumes on AD2 did **91,000** and **107,000**. The other is new setting topics
with no curiosity attached — storms, **44,705**; home invasions, **40,518**; fishing, **64,708**.
Fourteen of its fifteen weakest videos are from this year.

The exception is *Things on Asian Internet*, which nearly doubled on its second volume — and the
other language lanes didn't repeat it: *Hispanic Internet* did **40,671**.

### What I'd change

*[SLIDE: four-changes.png]*

**One.** Enforce the three-test filter at greenlight, in writing — every no recorded with its
reason.

**Two.** A series gets a next volume only if its last one beat the channel's average. Otherwise it's
retired.

**Three.** Rotate three title frameworks, drop the "Vol." numbers, and keep "Iceberg" for one or
two premium uploads per channel a month. "Dashcam Vol. 5" becomes "The Dashcam Footage From Russia
Nobody Can Explain."

**Four.** End every segment on the document that already existed, so depth comes from the record
instead of from volume. That's the basis of all three pitches.

One through three are testable inside two months, against today's floor of 133 to 141 thousand a
video, channel by channel. Each test gets a written prediction before it ships and a review after —
that's the start of the playbook.

#### A format worth testing

*[SLIDE: next-format.png]*

One thing to test, not roll out: **one case, seven levels, 25 to 35 minutes** — depth means further
into one incident, not more incidents. It removes the fifty-clip constraint and lands near the
28-minute sweet spot. The risk is that if the case doesn't hold, nothing else carries the video.
One video, one channel, one month, against that channel's baseline.

## QUESTION 2 — THREE PITCHES

### What makes it an Abyssal video

*[SLIDE: five-invariants.png]*

Of the five invariants, **forbidden or obscure** is the product — the other four are quality
control on it. And topic novelty is close to gone: Kitchen Nightmares, child stars, India and
Telegram are all covered already — the first two by videos with millions of views. So these pitches
apply one change to the format rather than chase untouched subjects.

### The change: every segment ends on the paperwork

*[SLIDE: the-document-axis.png]*

**Every segment ends on the document** — the inspection that was failed, the report that was filed,
the check that was run and ignored.

Levels still escalate on harm. Making each level a stage of the paperwork — nobody knew at Level 1,
they knew and signed it off at Level 7 — doesn't fill: the spec needs five to seven cases per level,
and there's one Mottarone, not six. So the document is the **signature of every case**, not the
definition of the level.

What it gets you: the surface can be familiar, because the depth is the report — and inquiry
findings rarely make it onto YouTube. The moral frame becomes structural. It escalates harder than
a body count. A solo channel can't easily copy it. "The document we couldn't put on YouTube" is a
stronger paid layer than "Level 8." And it's measurable where it matters most here: retention at
level transitions.

It fits two channels, because they sit inside mandatory inspection regimes. AO doesn't, so it gets
a different answer. The case counts are sized targets: the anchors are verified, and building the
full inventory is week-one work.

### Pitch 1 · Abyssal Detective — "the Most Disturbing Structural Collapses Iceberg"

*[SLIDE: pitch-collapses.png]*

**The document: the engineering report.**

Disasters are an AD lane, and this one's hook is the collapse, not a footage format — so it's AD,
not AD2. AD's disaster lane has covered explosions, coasters, mountains, water parks, theme parks and
the sea, but never structures that came down with people inside — and it's one of the most-filmed
disaster categories there is.

**Framework.** Seven levels, about 44 cases, 55 minutes, escalating on scale and on who was inside.
Level 1 is cranes, scaffolding and facades — spectacular footage, small tolls. Level 2, buildings
that fell mid-construction, onto the people building them. Level 3, bridges: Genoa's Morandi
bridge, **43 dead**, and the FIU footbridge in Miami, **6 dead** — both on camera. Level 4, public
venues: **Hyatt Regency, 114 dead** — a walkway redesign approved over the phone that doubled the
load on a connection nobody recalculated. Level 5, occupied homes: **Surfside, 98 dead** — a 2018
engineering report describing major structural damage, nearly three years before. Level 6,
industrial: **Rana Plaza, 1,134 dead** — cracks reported the day before, workers ordered back in.
Level 7, dams and tailings, where the toll stops being countable. Level 8, on Patreon, is the reports
themselves.

**Hook.** A fixed security camera on an apartment building at night. Nothing happens. Then half of
it drops. Cut to black. *"Nearly three years before this, an engineer wrote a report about this
building. The structural repairs still hadn't started."*

**What I'd change.** Every segment closes on the report, not the death toll — so the video has a
repeating beat instead of reading as a list.

**Why it beats the original.** The nearest existing video is a general "Devastating Tragedies"
iceberg at **432,000**, and the single-collapse explainers stop at the cause — none asks who
already knew. Against AD's own disaster icebergs: a footage iceberg promises a clip you haven't
seen; this promises the ceiling above your head, and then shows it was written down.

**Monetization.** No gore; it's dust and distance. Spread across a dozen countries, no religion, no
politics. The cleanest of the three to ship.

### Pitch 2 · Abyssal Detective 2 — "the Most Disturbing Lift & Cable Failures Iceberg"

*[SLIDE: pitch-lifts.png]*

**The document: the inspection certificate.**

It's AD2's territory by either definition — accidents that were investigated once and then
forgotten, told through the footage. Elevators alone won't fill it — about **30 US deaths a year**,
nearly half maintenance workers in shafts, almost never filmed. So the scope is every machine that
lifts people under mandatory inspection: elevators, escalators, funiculars, cable cars, ski lifts,
mine hoists. Better footage, and no two segments look alike.

**Framework.** Seven levels, about 40 cases, 48 minutes, escalating on harm. Level 1, malfunctions
where nobody's hurt. Level 2, entrapments — hours in a sealed box, on camera. Level 3, survivors: in
Santiago, **José Vergara Acevedo fell 31 floors in 15 seconds** and lived. Level 4, injuries:
**Rome, 2018** — an escalator accelerated under a crowd of CSKA Moscow fans: **24 injured**, seven
seriously, one man partially lost a foot, and **eleven people were indicted in 2023**, with charges
including fraud. Level 5, single deaths: **Samuel Waisbren, 30** — the door-zone restrictor was
disabled, the building had been **fined $1,300 for exactly that**, and it was still unfixed. And
**JauMarcus McFarland, 18** — the elevator's operating permit had expired a year earlier. Level 6,
several deaths in one failure. Level 7, mass casualty: **Stresa–Mottarone, 2021** — the cable
snapped and the emergency brake never engaged, because a clamp had been fitted to hold it open,
routinely, to avoid downtime. **14 dead.** And **Vaal Reefs, 1995** — a locomotive fell down a mine
shaft onto a crowded cage. **104 dead.**

**Hook.** *"Somewhere in your building there's an inspection certificate for the elevator you use
every day. You've never once looked at it. Every machine in this video had one too."*

**What I'd change.** The machine stops being the subject. A footage iceberg says "look what happened
on camera"; this says "look what the certificate said, and who signed it." Same footage, and it
turns a compilation into an argument.

**Why it beats the original.** No lift or elevator iceberg exists — what's out there is news clips
and compilations with no story. Every segment here gets a cause and a piece of paper. And it gives
AD2 a repeatable line: machines under mandatory inspection. Lifts, then boilers and pressure vessels,
then cranes.

**Monetization.** Nothing at the deep end needs to be shown — CCTV cut before impact, named victims
described, never shown. Patreon gets the uncut footage and the certificates.

### Pitch 3 · Abyssal Observer — "the Most Disturbing Reality TV Casting Iceberg"

*[SLIDE: pitch-casting.png]*

**AO gets a different answer.** TV shows don't sit inside an inspection regime, and the obvious
version — Kitchen Nightmares through health inspection files — doesn't hold: those records are
typically kept about **three years**, and the show ran 2007 to 2014. One show also can't fill seven
levels.

**The topic: the people reality TV put on screen without checking who they were. The document: the
background check.**

**Framework.** Seven levels, about 42 cases, one person each, 50 minutes, escalating on the
seriousness of the offence. Every case has broadcast footage, drawn from US and UK shows so it isn't
built on one legal system. Level 1, financial crime: **Todd and Julie Chrisley** — sentenced to
twelve and seven years for bank fraud and tax evasion, then pardoned in 2025 — and the Giudices.
Level 2, drugs and driving offences. Level 3, domestic violence. Level 4, violent crime: **Geoffrey
Paschel**, *90 Day Fiancé* — 18 years for aggravated kidnapping and domestic assault, arrested a year
before his season aired. Level 5, sexual offences: **Stephen Bear**, convicted of voyeurism and
sharing private sexual images. Level 6, offences against children: **Josh Duggar**. Level 7, murder:
**James Timothy Norman**, *Welcome to Sweetie Pie's* — life in prison for a federal murder-for-hire
plot against his own nephew. And **Ryan Jenkins**, cast on VH1's *Megan Wants a Millionaire* — the
background check came back clean and missed his **2007 conviction in Canada for assaulting a
girlfriend**. Months later he was wanted for the murder of his wife, Jasmine Fiore. VH1 pulled the
series.

**Hook.** His intro from the show — confident, likeable — held a beat too long. *"The producers paid
for a background check on this man. It came back clean. It had missed one thing — a conviction for
assaulting a girlfriend, one border away. Months later, he was wanted for murder."*

**What I'd change.** AO's named-show icebergs start from a show and look for the consequences inside
it. This starts from the consequence and uses the show as the way in — AO's own rule applied
literally.

**Why it beats the original.** What exists is single-case news coverage and generic
celebrities-in-prison lists; nobody has built an ordered descent with casting as the through-line.
Against AO's named-show icebergs — one show, many incidents — this is one failure mode across many
shows, which is what lets it go deeper than any single show can. And it's built on AO's strongest
pattern: TV and reality topics with a real consequence.

**Monetization.** Done badly this is a gawk-at-criminals list; done right, the institution on trial
is the casting process. No crime-scene material needed; Patreon gets the court filings themselves.
**Level 6 is the exception** — offences against children are the hardest category on the platform.
My call: two sentences per case, charge and sentence only, nothing extra behind the paywall — and if
the limited-ads rate says otherwise after one video, cut the level.

### CLOSE

The format isn't failing because it got old. It's failing because the runtime took over topic
selection, the filter that's already written down stopped being applied, and series kept getting
volumes after the data said stop. Enforce the filter, make every series earn its next volume, test
the titles — and compete on depth instead of topic novelty, with every segment ending on the
document.

Thanks for watching.

### The title-monoculture hole, and how the JD fixes it

The standing problem from the strategy review: 98% of titles have *always* used the formula —
including the 4.03M and 3.17M peaks — so the formula is a constant and cannot explain a change.

The JD supplies a better diagnostic instrument. Its own topic filter:

> "Each one passes three tests: **is there real depth here** (enough verified material for a
> full ladder), **does the surface level pull a click** from someone who has never heard of us,
> and **does the descent go somewhere the audience cannot easily find on their own.** All three
> must be yes."

Run the catalogue through their three tests and the decline explains itself without any shaky
causal claim:

- **Early topics** — Footage Caught on Camera (4.03M), CCTV (3.17M), Internet Mysteries (2.2M) —
  pass all three. Deep verified material, instantly legible surface, and a descent most viewers
  genuinely cannot self-serve.
- **Recent topics** — Biblical Horrors (60K), Amazon Incidents (71K), Fishing Encounters (65K),
  Chinese Shows (54K) — fail test 1 and test 3. There isn't a full ladder of verified material,
  and what depth exists is a search away.

That is topic exhaustion stated in their own framework, it fits the shape of the curve, and it
needs no claim that anything changed about the titles. Titles then become what they actually
are: **the highest-leverage variable nobody has tested**, a ceiling constraint rather than the
cause of the fall — and the one the strategist owns outright.

### 2B · The format spec — and all three pitches fail it

### 2C · The segment bar, and the footage-anchor rule that kills Hotels

> **§1.5:** "Every segment needs **30 or more seconds of usable footage or recording**, a clear
> 60 to 120 second story structure, a genuinely disturbing element, and evergreen relevance.
> **Segment killers: photos only**, brief viral clips with no story, dead trends, segments too

Running Hotels through this is fatal:

| Hotels case | Footage? |
|---|---|
| H.H. Holmes (1893) | ❌ photos only |
| Winecoff fire (1946) | ❌ largely stills |
| Erin Andrews | ❌ footage exists but is non-consensual nudity — unusable |
| Cottingham · I-65 Killer · Craigslist Killer | ❌ true crime, no footage anchor |
| Days Inn / Georgia / Philadelphia trafficking | ❌ court records, no footage |
| Cecil Hotel · MGM Grand · Hyatt aftermath | ✅ |

Hotels is mostly **court records and history with no footage** — rejected network-wide. It was

Kitchen Nightmares has a milder version of the same problem: the deportation, Amy's conviction

Elevators clears this bar best: CCTV coverage is the entire premise, and the segment supply is

> **§1.2:** "That is the format we run today, and it is also our problem: it is aging. The

measures is unanswered.** Q1's "what would you change" is asking, at least in part, for a

without padding").

This also retires the "format is not aging" line for good: they state twice that it is aging.
The job is to say what replaces it.

### 2E · §1.1 answers "what makes it an Abyssal video" — and audits the pitches

> **§1.1 — the five invariants:** the material is **real and verified**; it is **disturbing but
> survivable** (the guide takes the hit); it feels **forbidden or obscure, something the viewer
> could not or should not find alone**; it carries a **moral frame** (victims honored,
> perpetrators judged, institutions held to account); it is delivered by a **human guide**.

That is the answer to Q2's unanswered sub-question — quote it, then show each pitch against it.

Applying invariant three is uncomfortable for Kitchen Nightmares: **3.93M people watched the
closure story three months ago.** It is neither forbidden nor obscure. The pitch survives only
if levels 4–7 carry material that genuinely isn't self-servable — the criminal records and the

Also worth using as the Q1 spine — §1.1's "**the viewer gets their morbid curiosity closed just

Internet Mysteries all had it. Biblical Horrors, Amazon Incidents, Fishing Encounters, Chinese
Shows never did — there was no itch to scratch, so nothing got closed.

## How the analysis was done

Channel data came from the applicant's own CSV exports of all three channels plus YouTube's
public figures, reconciled month by month. The cadence analysis recomputes Pearson correlations
across 61 channel-months and re-runs them restricted to 2026 to control for time. Novelty checks
ran against the full 330-video topic inventory and then against YouTube search per topic. External
facts were verified against primary reporting, court records and regulatory sources — every one
is cited in Part 5.

Where a number could not be verified, it was cut rather than softened. Where a conclusion was
contradicted by its own data, the reversal was kept in the script rather than removed.
