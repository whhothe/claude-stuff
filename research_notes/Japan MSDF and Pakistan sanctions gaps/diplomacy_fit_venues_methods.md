# Diplomacy-studies fit, publication venues, and process-tracing exemplars for two Japan thesis topics

Scope note: research conducted 2026-11-11 with web search (18 tool calls). www.tandfonline.com could not be fetched from this session (host not on the network allowlist), so Taylor & Francis journal pages (Diplomacy & Statecraft, Pacific Review, Asian Security, Japan Forum, Asian Affairs, Strategic Analysis, Contemporary South Asia, JCA) were NOT opened directly; journal-level claims below that lack a link are flagged as background knowledge, not verified. Topic 1 = Japan's 2019 independent MSDF Middle East dispatch / Abe's 2019 Tehran visit. Topic 2 = Japan's 1998 nuclear-test sanctions on Pakistan, October 2001 lifting, 2005 yen-loan resumption.

## (a) How do diplomacy texts define the relevant sub-fields, and which topic more squarely counts as "diplomacy"?

### Takeaway
Topic 1 maps onto several named diplomacy sub-fields at once (mediation / head-of-government diplomacy via Abe's Tehran trip; cooperative/persuasive maritime diplomacy per Le Mière; alliance management; hedging), but its core act - a Cabinet decision to deploy warships and P-3Cs under a "survey and research" legal basis - is equally (arguably more) a security/defence-policy decision. Topic 2 is a textbook case of economic statecraft / aid-sanctions diplomacy in service of nuclear non-proliferation diplomacy (Baldwin's "economic statecraft"; Bayne & Woolcock's economic diplomacy), and its sequence (impose -> "suspend" -> resume loans tied to counter-terrorism partnership) is negotiated state-to-state signalling, so it sits squarely within diplomacy-as-statecraft, although some would file it under foreign economic/aid policy. Judgement: Topic 2 fits "diplomacy" more cleanly as a single sub-field (sanctions/aid diplomacy); Topic 1 fits "diplomacy" only if framed around the diplomatic choreography (Tehran visit, Rouhani's December 2019 Tokyo visit, the explicit "not joining but cooperating" message to Washington) rather than the deployment itself.

### Inferences
- Background-knowledge definitions (NOT verified in this session; student should check exact wording and edition):
  - Berridge, *Diplomacy: Theory and Practice* (Palgrave Macmillan; 6th ed. 2022) treats diplomacy as the conduct of international relations by negotiation and other peaceful means, principally by professional diplomats; has chapters on summitry and mediation. Abe's Tehran trip fits Berridge's summitry/mediation chapters; the MSDF deployment does not obviously fit Berridge's core definition.
  - Cooper, Heine & Thakur (eds), *The Oxford Handbook of Modern Diplomacy* (OUP, 2013) contains chapters on economic diplomacy, sanctions, mediation, defence/military diplomacy and nuclear diplomacy; its broad "new diplomacy" framing accommodates both topics.
  - Bayne & Woolcock (eds), *The New Economic Diplomacy* (Ashgate then Routledge; 4th ed. 2017) define economic diplomacy as decision-making, policy-making and advocacy in international economic relations; sanctions and aid are borderline in their framing (they emphasise negotiation over coercion), so Topic 2 is better anchored in Baldwin.
  - Baldwin, *Economic Statecraft* (Princeton UP, 1985; new edition 2020) treats economic statecraft (including aid suspension = negative sanction, aid resumption = positive sanction/inducement) as one of the techniques of statecraft alongside diplomacy (narrowly defined), propaganda and military statecraft. Important nuance: in Baldwin's typology "diplomacy" is a *separate* technique from economic statecraft, so strictly Topic 2 is "economic statecraft" not "diplomacy"; in broader diplomacy-studies usage (e.g., Oxford Handbook) sanctions are a diplomatic instrument.
  - Cable, *Gunboat Diplomacy* (1971; 3rd ed. 1994 as *Gunboat Diplomacy 1919-1991*) defines gunboat diplomacy as the use or threat of limited naval force, otherwise than as an act of war, to secure advantage or avert loss. Japan's 2019 mission was explicitly non-coercive and avoided Hormuz, so it is NOT gunboat/coercive diplomacy; at most Le Mière's "cooperative" or "persuasive" maritime diplomacy (signalling commitment to the US and to shipping security while reassuring Iran).
- Classification summary:
  - Topic 1: primary = alliance management / hedging (middle-power) + head-of-government mediation diplomacy; secondary = cooperative/persuasive maritime (naval) diplomacy and energy-security diplomacy. Defence diplomacy in Cottey & Forster's sense (peacetime *cooperative* use of armed forces with partners) fits only weakly because the deployment was unilateral rather than cooperative with a partner military — a point the student can turn into a conceptual contribution ("unilateral naval presence as diplomatic signalling").
  - Topic 2: primary = sanctions/aid diplomacy (negative then positive economic statecraft) within nuclear non-proliferation diplomacy; secondary = alliance management (alignment with US post-9/11 lifting) and coercive diplomacy (conditionality on CTBT). Coercive diplomacy in the George sense requires a demand plus threat; Japan's CTBT condition qualifies loosely.
  - Topic 2 sits more squarely inside "diplomacy" as a recognised category in diplomacy-studies journals (sanctions and aid are standard chapters), while Topic 1's core decision is commonly treated in the security-policy literature (SDF dispatch, Article 9, 2015 legislation).

## (b) Realistic publication venues: scope, length, single-country case studies, and Japan–Middle East / Japan–South Asia track record

### Takeaway
Best-fit diplomacy-branded venues: The Hague Journal of Diplomacy (Brill, practice-of-diplomacy focus; publishes single-country practice studies) and Diplomacy & Statecraft (T&F; historical/archival diplomacy, very receptive to Topic 2 as a 1998–2005 diplomatic-history case). Best Asia-regional venues: Asian Survey, The Pacific Review, Asian Security (Topic 1), International Relations of the Asia-Pacific, Japanese Journal of Political Science, Japan Forum, Contemporary Japan. For Topic 2 a South Asia venue (Strategic Analysis, Contemporary South Asia, Asian Affairs) or the Nonproliferation Review is also plausible. Directly comparable precedents verified: JJPS (Iraq SDF dispatch process), Asian Survey (Arabian Sea SDF dispatch), Japan Forum (Japanese policy process under foreign pressure), Pacific Review (Japan ODA/foreign policy to Ukraine 2014–2023; Sino-Japanese aid suspension), Social Science Japan Journal (Japan aid sanctions on China), Nonproliferation Review (1998 South Asia sanctions).

## (c) Process-tracing methodology references and published exemplars

### Takeaway
Core method texts are verified (Collier 2011; Beach & Pedersen 2013/2019; Bennett & Checkel 2015). Verified exemplars applicable as models: Japanese decision-process studies of SDF dispatches (JJPS 2006 Iraq; Asian Survey 2003 Arabian Sea), Japanese aid-sanction decision-making (Katada 2001), a Tübingen dissertation using explicit process-tracing on Koizumi-era foreign policy, a Springer volume tracing post-2001 Japanese foreign-policy decisions, and an explicit process-tracing study of sanctions imposition-and-relief (Raynor 2022, EJIR). Explicit "process tracing" labelling is rare in the Japan-specific works; most use decision-process narratives, so the student's contribution can be methodological rigor (Van Evera tests / Bayesian updating) applied to a known-but-under-theorised case.
