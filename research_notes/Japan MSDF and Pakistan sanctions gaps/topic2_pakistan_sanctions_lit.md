# Japan's 1998 sanctions on Pakistan, the 26 Oct 2001 lifting, and the 2005 resumption of yen loans: literature and gap assessment

Research method note (read first): searches ran through a web-search tool in Nov 2026. Direct page fetches to mofa.go.jp, jica.go.jp, piie.com and sujo.usindh.edu.pk were **blocked by this session's network allowlist** (DNS failure). So several items below were confirmed from search-index snippets and catalogue records, not full text. Each item has a status tag:

## Q1. What peer-reviewed scholarship analyses Japan's 1998 sanctions on India and Pakistan and their lifting?

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed work that treats the 1998–2001 Japanese measures **as its main object** appears to be almost nonexistent. The episode shows up as (a) one case or table row in broader studies of the ODA Charter and aid sanctions (Hoshiro 2022; DiFilippo 2003; Nikitina and Furuoka), (b) a passage in Japan–India or Japan–South Asia studies (Jain ISAS 2007; Jain JICA DP 45/46, 2026; Horimoto; Ito 2007), or (c) a paragraph in Pakistani descriptive surveys (Malik 2008; Abbasi and Khatwani 2013; Asia Pacific 2008). I found no stand-alone article on the Pakistan case in English, Japanese or Pakistani journals.

### Inferences
- The literature splits along three lines. **Charter/sanctions** scholars (Katada, Furuoka, Hoshiro, DiFilippo) treat Japan's decision-making but use China as the main case, with India/Pakistan as a data point. **South Asia regionalists** (Jain, Horimoto, Ito) focus on India and nuclear politics. **Pakistani bilateral surveys** (Malik, Abbasi, APRJ) are descriptive and repeat the "9/11 → lifting" narrative without sources on the Japanese side.
- No item found uses Japanese-language primary sources (Diet records, MOFA evaluation reports) to reconstruct the Pakistan decision chain.

### Gaps
- Full texts of Hoshiro 2022, Jain DP46, Malik 2008 and the Nikitina/Furuoka paper were not read, so I cannot exclude that one of them gives a paragraph-level explanation of the 2001–05 lag. These four should be read in full before claiming a gap.
- CiNii, JSTOR and Google Scholar could not be queried directly. The negative result for Japanese articles needs confirmation.

## Q2. How do these works explain the lifting, and is Pakistan treated separately from India? Is the 2001-to-2005 lag explained?

### Takeaway
Nearly every source explains the lifting by **9/11 and Pakistan's role in the anti-terror coalition**, often framed as US-driven (DiFilippo, Ito, Abbasi, APRJ, PIIE). Official Japanese documents add **nonproliferation progress** (the test moratoria, export controls) and the need for mid- to long-term support for Pakistan. Pakistan and India are almost always **lumped together**. The partial exceptions note the asymmetric impact on Pakistan (Jain DP46; the Muslim Asia chapter) and India's earlier thaw (Jain ISAS 2007). The **2001–2005 lag in new yen loans is explained only in Japanese government evaluation documents**, which cite concern over Pakistan's debt-bearing capacity. I found no scholarly analysis of the lag.

### Cited Findings
- **MOFA, 26 Oct 2001:** Japan "highly values the efforts of India and Pakistan to contribute to strengthening the international coalition against terrorism" and decided to "discontinue" the measures. Lifting is framed as a suspension that could be reinstated if the test moratoria were broken — [MOFA press conference 26 Oct 2001](https://www.mofa.go.jp/announce/press/2001/10/1026.html) (content via search snippets); [VOA 26 Oct 2001](https://www.voanews.com/a/a-13-a-2001-10-26-27-japan-66408297/549447.html); [CNN 26 Oct 2001](https://edition.cnn.com/2001/WORLD/asiapcf/east/10/26/japan.sanctions/index.html). CNN reports a US$40m pledge to Pakistan and calls the move "controversial in Japan".
- **ODA White Paper 2002** (Japanese): the decision followed comprehensive consideration of progress in both countries' nuclear disarmament and nonproliferation efforts and the need for mid- to long-term support to Pakistan. Measures could be revived if the nonproliferation situation worsened — [MOFA ODA白書2002 インド・パキスタン](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/oda/shiryo/hakusyo/02_hakusho/ODA2002/html/honpen/hp2030200.htm).
- After lifting, Japan announced US$300m in grant aid for Pakistan — [MOFA ODA White Paper 2002 (English)](https://www.mofa.go.jp/policy/oda/white/2002/part1_2_2_3.html); [MOFA Pakistan evaluation report ch.2](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/oda/shiryo/hyouka/kunibetu/gai/pakistan/kn03_01_0201.html).
- **Lag explanation (key finding):** a JICA report says that after the October 2001 lifting, new grant aid resumed, but **new yen loans stayed suspended because of concern over Pakistan's debt-bearing capacity** (債務負担能力への懸念). The same report says the capacity had "greatly improved" because of several changes in the situation. Post-2001 yen lending was limited to continuing projects: Kohat Tunnel (II) and (III), about ¥4.0bn in FY2001 and about ¥3.1bn committed in 2002 — [JICA report 第Ⅲ部 パキスタンに対する援助の実績と評価](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737731_05.pdf); [第Ⅰ部 総論](https://openjicareport.jica.go.jp/pdf/11737731_02.pdf). (These appear to be a JICA country assistance study or evaluation of Pakistan, c. 2003–04. Exact title and year not captured.)
- **Rationale for resumption:** in August 2004 the government judged that Japan should support Pakistan's stable development over the mid to long term to correct poverty and social disparities that breed terrorism. In April 2005 it announced resumed yen loans (Lower Chenab Canal rehabilitation; Load Dispatch System upgrade) and decided both in August 2005 — [MOFA ODA databook, Pakistan](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/oda/shiryo/kuni/05_databook/pdfs/02-04.pdf); [ODA白書2004 パキスタン](https://www.mofa.go.jp/mofaj/gaiko/oda/shiryo/hakusyo/04_hakusho/ODA2004/html/honpen/hp202050200.htm).
- Koizumi's 30 April 2005 Islamabad visit signalled the resumption, citing Pakistan's "vital role" against terrorism. Reported scale: about US$160m. Koizumi also raised the A.Q. Khan network — [Al Jazeera, 1 May 2005](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2005/5/1/japan-premier-wraps-up-pakistan-visit); [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/266209).
- **Asymmetric impact:** sanctions hurt Pakistan more than India because of Pakistan's deeper dependence on Japanese ODA — [Jain DP46](https://www.jica.go.jp/english/jica_ri/publication/discussion/__icsFiles/afieldfile/2026/02/26/Discussion_Paper_No46.pdf); [Muslim Asia chapter](https://www.academia.edu/95463611/Japan_s_Relations_with_Muslim_Asia_Trans_Continental_Normativity_and_Policy). DP46 also reportedly notes that Japan continued some project loans to India during the sanctions.
- **India thaw before 9/11:** Mori's August 2000 India visit smoothed ties — [Jain ISAS WP41](https://www.isas.nus.edu.sg/papers/41-from-condemnation-to-strategic-partnership-japanocos-changing-view-of-india-1998-2007).
- **Conflicting dating:** ISSI dates the lifting to 2005 — [ISSI](https://issi.org.pk/the-pakistan-japan-ties/). This contradicts MOFA, VOA, CNN and the JICA reports, which all give 26 Oct 2001.
- **Extra Pakistan-specific layer:** PIIE also codes a separate coup case (Musharraf, Oct 1999) with Japan as a sender. That makes Pakistan's sanction regime "nuclear plus democracy", which India's was not (see Q3).

### Inferences
- Four candidate mechanisms for the lifting appear in the literature, but none has been tested against evidence: (1) **US alignment/gaiatsu** after 9/11 (DiFilippo; Pakistani surveys); (2) **counterterrorism** as an autonomous Japanese priority (MOFA's coalition language; the "poverty breeds terrorism" rationale of 2004); (3) **nonproliferation conditionality met** (moratoria, export controls; ODA White Paper 2002); (4) **India commercial pull** (Jain; India's earlier thaw). Pakistan was lifted simultaneously with India, possibly as a "package". Whether Pakistan rode India's coat-tails, or the reverse, is unexamined.
- **The lag is the clearest publishable gap.** The only available explanation (debt-bearing capacity) is an official one. It has not been examined against alternatives: Paris Club rescheduling (Dec 2001), IMF PRGF conditionality, MOF/JBIC creditor caution versus MOFA political preference, LDP politics, the A.Q. Khan revelations (Feb 2004), and the timing of Koizumi's 2005 South Asia trip, which paired Pakistan with India. The fact that grants resumed in 2001 and loans in 2005 lets a researcher separate MOFA (grant) logic from MOF/JBIC (loan) logic, which suits process tracing well.
- Pakistan is rarely treated separately. The asymmetry (higher dependence, the coup layer, the debt crisis, the loan lag) is noted but not theorised.

### Gaps
- I could not verify any LDP- or Diet-level account of who pushed for lifting or for the 2005 resumption.
- No source found weighs debt-capacity concerns against nonproliferation hesitancy (A.Q. Khan, 2004) as the cause of the lag.

## Q3. What do the PIIE sanctions cases and other databases say?

### Takeaway
In Hufbauer, Schott, Elliott and Oegg (*Economic Sanctions Reconsidered*, 3rd ed., 2007), **Case 99-3 is "US, Japan v. Pakistan (1999–2001: Coup, restore democracy)"**. It is the post-Musharraf-coup case and cross-references the nuclear case. The nuclear sanctions sit under the long-running US nonproliferation case **79-2** (US v. Pakistan). I found no separate PIIE case in which Japan is the primary sender for the 1998 nuclear measures. TIES or GSDB coding was not verified.

### Cited Findings
- PIIE Case 99-3 summary (via search snippet): Japan had cut off roughly US$300–500m a year in aid and loans after the 1998 tests. The US took no further punitive step after the coup because 1998 sanctions were already in place. Japan lifted its Pakistan sanctions in October 2001 to back the US-led campaign against terrorism — [PIIE Case 99-3](https://www.piie.com/commentary/speeches-papers/case-99-3).
- Case 79-2 covers the US response to the 1998 tests (Glenn Amendment) and the threat to Pakistan — [PIIE Case 79-2](https://www.piie.com/commentary/speeches-papers/case-79-2).
- US-side context: [CRS 98-570, India-Pakistan Nuclear Tests and U.S. Response](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/98-570.html); [CRS RS20995](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20030203_RS20995_b53ff192a8d291c9124e2460febad5356c3ee68d.html); [Carnegie, "Pakistan's Sanction Waivers: A Summary" (Oct 2001)](https://carnegieendowment.org/2001/10/29/pakistan-s-sanction-waivers-summary-pub-10778); [CNN, US lifts final sanctions on Pakistan, 30 Oct 2001](https://www.cnn.com/2001/US/10/29/gen.us.pak.sanctions/index.html).

### Inferences
- Because PIIE files Japan's role under the coup case and folds the nuclear sanctions into a US-led case, standard sanctions datasets **do not treat Japan as an independent sender** with its own motives. That is a framing a new study could correct.

### Gaps
- PIIE pages could not be fetched (host blocked), so the outcome scores (policy result, sanctions contribution, success score) for 99-3 and the Japan elements of 79-2 were **not retrieved**. Whether an India nuclear case number (e.g., a 1974/1998 India case) lists Japan is also unverified.
- **TIES (Threat and Imposition of Economic Sanctions, Morgan/Bapat/Kobayashi)** and **GSDB (Global Sanctions Data Base, Felbermayr et al.)** almost certainly include Japan–Pakistan 1998 observations given their coverage, but I did **not verify** the case IDs or coding. Check the TIES 4.0 case file and GSDB directly.

## Q4. What primary sources are available?

### Takeaway
The primary record is rich and mostly online: MOFA statements (1998, 2001), ODA White Papers and annual reports (1998–2006), MOFA and JICA country evaluations, the 2005 Country Assistance Program, the JBIC 10 Aug 2005 release, IDE chronologies, worldjpn.net statements, US CRS reports, Dawn archives and WikiLeaks Tokyo cables. **Diet minutes (kokkai.ndl.go.jp) were not searchable from here** but are the obvious untapped source.

### Cited Findings

## Q5. Are there explicit gaps or calls for research, and has anyone used process tracing?

### Takeaway
I found **no study that uses process tracing (or any explicit causal method) on Japan's 1998–2005 Pakistan decisions**, and no explicit call for research on this specific episode. The nearest general calls are Malik's reviewer calling the bilateral literature "thin", and Hoshiro's finding that Charter norms do not predict aid decisions, which invites case studies of when norms were and were not applied.

### Cited Findings
- Malik (2008) is described as the first book-length study of Pakistan–Japan relations. The review in *Contemporary South Asia* reportedly calls it "a valuable addition to a thin body of literature" — [Routledge listing](https://www.routledge.com/Pakistan-Japan-Relations-Continuity-and-Change-in-Economic-Relations-and/Malik/p/book/9780415462792) (quotation via search snippet; review not independently checked).
- Hoshiro (2022): the ODA Charter shaped allocation, but its norm-based principles had no measurable effect on aid decisions. The article notes inconsistent application across nuclear testers — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:sscijp:v:25:y:2022:i:2:p:297-330.); [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/ssjj/article/25/2/297/6623209).
- DiFilippo (2003) presents the lifting as Japan's anti-nuclear policy "missing its target", an inconsistency claim with no traced mechanism — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12964707).
- Katada (2001) supplies a decision-making framework for aid sanctions (China case) that has apparently not been applied to India/Pakistan — [citation record](https://library.fes.de/libalt/journals/swetsfulltext/14218850.PDF).

### Inferences — gap assessment
- **Gap 1 (strong): explaining the lifting with evidence.** Existing claims ("US pressure", "war on terror") are assertions. A process-tracing study could test competing hypotheses (gaiatsu vs. autonomous counterterrorism vs. nonproliferation conditionality met vs. India commercial pull vs. Pakistan collapse fear) using MOFA statements, Diet records, press and interviews.
- **Gap 2 (strongest, apparently unoccupied): the 2001–2005 loan lag.** The only explanation (debt-bearing capacity) is official and untested. Pakistan's grants-vs-loans split is analytically valuable because it separates MOFA grant logic from MOF/JBIC creditor logic. Rival explanations: A.Q. Khan revelations (2004), the coup/democracy conditionality (PIIE 99-3), and Koizumi's summit diplomacy timing.
- **Gap 3: Pakistan as a distinct case, not an India appendage.** Pakistan's higher dependence, the coup layer and the debt crisis make it a "most likely" case for sanctions working and a hard case for norm consistency.
- **Gap 4: Japan as an independent sender in sanctions datasets.** PIIE files Japan under US-led cases.
- **Publishability:** an article-length case study in *Social Science Japan Journal*, *Japanese Journal of Political Science*, *Asian Survey*, *Contemporary South Asia*, *Pacific Review* or *Asia Pacific Research Journal* seems realistic, provided it (a) engages Katada/Hoshiro/Furuoka on aid-sanction theory, (b) positions itself against Jain DP46 (2026) and Malik (2008), and (c) brings new Japanese-language primary evidence (Diet, JBIC, evaluation reports). The bilingual primary-source access of a researcher with lived experience in both countries is the comparative advantage.
- **Risk:** Jain DP46 (Feb 2026) and Malik (2008) were not read in full. If either explains the loan lag in depth, Gap 2 narrows. Read both first.

### Gaps
- Google Scholar "process tracing" + Japan + Pakistan + aid could not be run directly. Confirm the negative result.
- No Japanese 修士論文/博士論文 (master's or doctoral thesis) search was possible. Japanese university repositories (IRDB) may hold unpublished theses on this topic.
