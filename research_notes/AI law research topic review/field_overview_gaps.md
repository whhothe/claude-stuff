## Q1. What do bibliometric and systematic reviews (2023–2026) say about topic distribution and growth?

### Takeaway
The AI governance literature grew sharply from about 2017 and surged in the 2020s. Its centre of gravity is principles, ethics, fairness and privacy, and EU AI Act framework analysis, all mostly normative or conceptual. Empirical, lifecycle-specific and deployment-stage work is a small share. Corporate safety research specifically has moved toward pre-deployment topics such as alignment and testing and evaluation (T&E), away from deployment-stage issues such as bias.

### Cited Findings
**General bibliometric and systematic reviews**
- A Web of Science bibliometric review searched "(AI OR 'artificial intelligence') AND (governance OR regulation)" in October 2024 and found about 6,000 studies. After de-duplication and refinement, 510 unique publications were specific to EU AI Act governance, described as "about 7% of AI governance literature". The paper says AI governance literature "began to be published in significant numbers from 2017 onwards, with a notable surge… in the 2020s". Its keyword network centres on education, ethics and ChatGPT. It concludes that research on systems regulated by the AI Act "remains relatively limited compared to the broader AI research landscape" (snippet-verified). The paper also appeared as an AIES 2025 extended abstract — [arXiv 2502.03468](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.03468); [AIES extended abstract](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AIES/article/view/36642)
- Batool, Zowghi & Bano, "AI governance: a systematic literature review", *AI and Ethics* (2025), DOI 10.1007/s43681-024-00653-w. The preprint analysed 61 studies, filtered from 2,918 papers at screening. It framed the field around who governs, what is governed, when in the lifecycle governance happens, and how. Only 5 of the 61 studies fully answered all of those questions. A central finding is that governance solutions lack evidence of effectiveness in practice. A Scribd copy reports 28 articles, so the counts may differ between versions; flag this — [arXiv 2401.10896](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10896v1); [regulations.ai listing](https://regulations.ai/research/paper-2025-ai-governance-a-systematic-literature-review)
- Gumbo & Booyse, "Regulation of Artificial Intelligence: A Systematic Literature Review", *Œconomica* (2025). It searched Scopus (65) and WoS (51) in December 2024, retrieved 116 records, and selected 38 articles from 2019–2024 under PRISMA. Its results were mapped with VOSviewer. The small final corpus shows how narrow "regulation"-keyword SLRs remain — [Danubius journal](https://dj.univ-danubius.ro/index.php/AUDOE/article/view/3190)
- A broader WoS study of 11,309 AI-governance articles (2015–2025) identifies five major research themes. The theme names were not visible in the extract. Separately, a WoS bibliometric study of 6,084 AI-ethics articles (2015–2025) reports these core themes: algorithmic fairness, privacy and data security, ethical governance of autonomous vehicles, and medical AI. Both are lower-tier venues; treat them as indicative only — [De Gruyter IJDLG 2025](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/ijdlg-2025-0007/html); [Manea et al. 2025](https://eia.feaa.ugal.ro/images/eia/2025_2/Manea_et_al.pdf)
- A Scopus bibliometric study of AI liability (2011–2024) found 154 documents and identifies AI rules and regulatory frameworks as an under-developed area within liability scholarship — [J. Intellectual Property Rights 2025](https://or.niscpr.res.in/index.php/JIPR/article/download/12285/5070/107654)

**Deployment versus pre-deployment research (the "real-world gaps" literature)**
- Strauss, Moure, O'Reilly & Rosenblat, "Real-World Gaps in AI Governance Research". The preprint is arXiv 2505.00174 (April 2025) and SSRC AI Disclosures Project working paper 2025-04. The peer-reviewed version is in *IEEE Access* Vol. 14 (2026), pp. 104060–104076, with a data deposit on IEEE DataPort. The paper analyses 1,178 safety and reliability papers drawn from 9,439 generative-AI papers, January 2020 to March 2025. It compares five corporate labs (Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI) with six universities (CMU, MIT, NYU, Stanford, UC Berkeley, UW). Its main finding is that corporate research "increasingly concentrates on pre-deployment areas — model alignment and testing & evaluation — while attention to deployment-stage issues such as model bias has waned" — [arXiv 2505.00174](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00174); [SSRC page](https://www.ssrc.org/publications/real-world-gaps-in-ai-governance-research/); [UJ repository listing IEEE Access citation](https://ujcontent.uj.ac.za/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Real-world-gaps-in-AI-governance-research/9963670907691); [IEEE DataPort data](https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/real-world-gaps-ai-governance-research-data)
- Within that paper (snippet-verified): only about 15–35% of T&E papers deal substantively with post-deployment issues, a range from LLM-assisted classification with o3 and Claude 3.7. Safety and reliability is 12% of academic papers against 16% of corporate papers, adjusted for authorship. Corporate work treats bias "as a pre-deployment model personality issue, rather than a post-deployment (practical) statistical issue". No exact figure for the decline in the bias share was found in the extracts — [arXiv 2505.00174](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.00174v1); [Hugging Face paper page](https://huggingface.co/papers/2505.00174)

**Governance documents, as distinct from scholarship: the MIT AI Risk Initiative mapping**
- MIT AI Risk Initiative, "Mapping the AI Governance Landscape: April 2026 Update" (9 April 2026). An LLM pipeline classifies more than 1,000 documents from CSET's AGORA archive against six taxonomies, covering risk domains, sectors, lifecycle stages, actors and others. The coverage patterns point to "potential gaps in governance coverage of socioeconomic risks, early lifecycle stages, and consumer-facing sectors". The authors caveat that further analysis is needed to judge which gaps matter most (snippet-verified) — [MIT AI Risk blog](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-april-2026-update); [CSET mirror](https://cset.georgetown.edu/article/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-april-2026-update/)
- The October 2025 pilot, classified with Claude Sonnet 4.5, found the least-covered risk subdomains to be AI welfare and rights, multi-agent risks, and economic and cultural devaluation of human effort. The least-covered sectors were accommodation and food services, arts and entertainment, and real estate. Planned next steps link the governance data to incident, mitigation and expert-assessment data — [AIGL summary of pilot](https://www.aigl.blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-pilot-test-and-update-oct-2025/); [MIT FutureTech](https://futuretech.mit.edu/publication/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape)

**Policy activity indicators (Stanford AI Index)**
- AI Index 2025 policy chapter: AI mentions in legislative proceedings across 75 countries rose 21.3% in 2024, from 1,557 in 2023 to 1,889, and have grown more than ninefold since 2016. The 2026 AI Index includes a Policy and Governance chapter, but its headline legislative counts were not retrieved. A figure of "47 countries with AI-specific legislation" attributed to the 2026 Index comes from an unreliable aggregator and is unverified — [AI Index 2025 policy chapter](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2025-ai-index-report/policy-and-governance); [AI Index 2026 policy chapter](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/policy-and-governance)

**Conference-level evidence**
- "Four Years of FAccT" (Laufer, Jain, Cooper, Kleinberg, Heidari; FAccT 2022) finds a disproportionate focus on a handful of narrow topics and a drift away from broader AI policy and governance. It predates 2023, so treat it as baseline context — [arXiv 2206.06738](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.06738v1.pdf)
- A 2026 preprint topic-models 3,550 papers: 1,770 AI-ethics papers, including the AIES and FAccT proceedings, and 1,780 AI-safety papers, retrieved to 9 July 2025. It maps risks and mitigations across the ethics and safety communities, and its code and data are public at github.com/gyevnarb/ai-safety-ethics. It is not law-specific — [arXiv 2603.14495](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.14495v1)

### Inferences
- The best-evidenced picture of what dominates is ethics and principles, fairness and bias, privacy, EU AI Act framework and compliance analysis, and generative AI/ChatGPT (education, copyright). Frontier-safety research (alignment, evals) dominates corporate output but is mostly technical rather than legal.
- Two kinds of mapping exist and should be kept apart. One maps scholarship (bibliometrics, the Strauss et al. paper corpus). The other maps governance instruments (MIT/AGORA). Both point the same way: deployment, socioeconomic and consumer-sector issues are under-covered relative to model-level, pre-deployment issues.
- Counts across bibliometric studies are not comparable, because search strings and databases (WoS versus Scopus) differ widely. A report should cite each with its own scope.

### Gaps
- No reliable SSRN download ranking for AI-law papers was found. SSRN top-download lists were not retrievable in this session.
- No 2024–2026 law-specific topic analysis of FAccT, AIES, CPDP or We Robot programmes was found. Conference-level topic shares therefore remain unquantified.
- The names of the five themes in the 11,309-article WoS study, and the exact bias-share decline in Strauss et al., could not be extracted.
- AI Index 2026 policy-chapter figures were not retrieved.

## Q2. What gaps do multiple reviews converge on?

### Takeaway
Independent reviews converge on five gaps. (1) Effectiveness and empirical evidence: almost no studies evaluate whether AI laws or soft-law instruments work. (2) Post-deployment and real-world monitoring, along with researcher access to deployment data. (3) Enforcement and compliance measurement. (4) Global South perspectives and capacity. (5) Operational detail for audits, sandboxes and incident reporting. The soft-law versus hard-law debate has largely settled on "complementary", but effectiveness evidence for either is thin.

### Cited Findings
**Effectiveness and empirical evaluation**
- Batool et al.'s SLR finds that governance solutions largely lack evidence or metrics of effectiveness in practice. Only 5 of 61 studies addressed all of who, what, when and how — [arXiv 2401.10896](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.10896v1)
- On EU regulatory sandboxes, Dardykina (2025, "Is There Enough Sand in the Sandbox?") says "there has never been a genuine effort to evaluate sandbox effectiveness". No dedicated systematic review measuring AI-sandbox effectiveness was found. The existing evidence is qualitative or practitioner-authored, such as CIPL (Oct 2025) and FARI (2025). The Datasphere Initiative (Feb 2025) counts 66 AI, data or tech sandboxes worldwide, 31 of them AI-specific, across 44 countries — [Osuva/UWasa repository](https://osuva.uwasa.fi/bitstreams/7708e599-06ef-4a25-8ed7-02a2ea0135e7/download); [CIPL 2025](https://www.informationpolicycentre.com/resources/learning-from-practice-designing-effective-regulatory-sandboxes/); [Datasphere](https://www.thedatasphere.org/datasphere-publish/sandboxes-for-ai/); [Regulatory Review, May 2026](https://www.theregreview.org/2026/05/28/gukal-will-ai-regulatory-sandboxes-work/)

**Post-deployment and real-world gaps**
- Strauss et al. flag high-risk deployment domains as under-researched: healthcare, finance and commerce, misinformation, persuasive and addictive features, hallucinations, and copyright in training and inference. They call for structured external access to deployment data and observability, and they suggest liability safe harbours to enable it — [arXiv 2505.00174](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.00174); [SSRC](https://www.ssrc.org/publications/real-world-gaps-in-ai-governance-research/)
- The MIT governance map finds governance documents thin on early lifecycle stages, socioeconomic risks and consumer-facing sectors. So the instrument-level gap partly overlaps the research-level gap, and partly differs from it, since MIT flags early-lifecycle stages as thin — [MIT AI Risk blog](https://airisk.mit.edu/blog/mapping-the-ai-governance-landscape-april-2026-update)

**Enforcement and compliance (empirical)**
- "Null Compliance" (Wright, Metcalf, Matias et al., FAccT 2024) is the canonical empirical compliance study. 155 student investigators examined 391 employers subject to NYC Local Law 144. Only 18 had posted bias-audit reports and 13 had posted transparency notices, roughly 5% and 3%. Because employers decide for themselves whether the law applies, non-compliance "cannot be ascertained" ("null compliance"). Nearly all posted audits reported impact ratios above 0.8 — [arXiv 2406.01399](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01399v1); [FAccT 2024 paper](https://facctconference.org/static/papers24/facct24-113.pdf)
- A companion paper, "Auditing Work" (Groves, Metcalf et al., FAccT 2024), studies LL144 auditors. It finds that audit work is largely about managing the relational dynamics the law sets up between stakeholders — [FAccT 2024](https://facctconference.org/static/papers24/facct24-74.pdf)

**AI auditing and third-party evaluation**
- Li & Goel (2025, *Information Systems Frontiers*) give an SLR of AI audits and auditability, covering audit scope, challenges, and auditability measures by responsible party — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/infosf/v27y2025i3d10.1007_s10796-024-10508-8.html)
- A scoping review of 58 AI audits finds that system-integration failures are under-explored. Audit independence is constrained by limited upstream access, and integrators often bear legal responsibility despite having little control. It is a preprint and not peer-reviewed — [arXiv 2608.04921](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.04921)
- "Audit Cards" (arXiv 2504.13839) analyses 24 frontier evaluation reports. It finds that key context is routinely missing, including auditor identity, resource and access level, and process integrity, and it proposes standardised reporting — [arXiv 2504.13839](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.13839v2)

**Global South**
- A 2026 preprint argues that the most consequential AI-accountability gap in the Global South "sits outside the mandate of every institution created in the current governance wave". It also says the International Network of AI Safety Institutes set no research directives for evaluation in the Global South. It is a preprint and not peer-reviewed — [arXiv 2607.21317](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.21317)
- A 2026 *AI & Society* synthesis of 50 articles (2019–2025) frames algorithmic and data colonialism. It is a critical synthesis, not an enforcement study. No systematic review empirically testing AI-law enforcement in the Global South was found — [Springer AI & Society](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-026-03153-z)

**Soft law versus hard law**
- ASU's Gutierrez & Marchant scoping review and database identified 634 AI soft-law programmes (2001–2019). 36% were created by the public sector, and 79% are principles, recommendations or strategies. Lack of enforceability is the central weakness, and "Soft Law 2.0" proposes 13 implementation mechanisms. The variable and theme counts differ between sources (107 versus 14 variables plus 93 themes) — [OECD.AI Wonk "Soft law 2.0"](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/soft-law-2-0); [Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-soft-law-is-used-in-ai-governance/); [ASU repository](https://repository.law.asu.edu/Repository/Gary-E-Marchant-6/Article/Soft-Law-An-Agile-and-Effective-Governance-Approach-for-Artificial-Intelligence-10292.html)
- An IEEE *Transactions on Technology and Society* special issue edited by Gutierrez, Marchant and Michael finds soft and hard law "complementary" rather than opposed — [technologyandsociety.org](https://technologyandsociety.org/?p=5896); [BIICL blog](https://www.biicl.org/blog/121/bridging-soft-and-hard-law-in-ai-governance)

### Inferences
- The strongest convergence across independent sources is on effectiveness and empirical evaluation (Batool et al.; Dardykina on sandboxes; soft-law enforceability; LL144 "null compliance") and on deployment-stage observability (Strauss et al.; audit-access literature). This is the safest "gap" claim for the final report.
- The soft-law/hard-law literature is mostly pre-2023 (data to 2019). Measuring how soft-law commitments, such as frontier-lab safety frameworks and the 2024 Seoul commitments, were turned into hard law by SB 53 and RAISE is an open and timely question.
- LL144 is the template for a feasible student study: a cheap, public-web compliance check of a disclosure-based law. SB 53 and RAISE are also disclosure-based (see Q3), so the same design transfers.

### Gaps
- No single meta-review quantifying how many AI-law papers are empirical versus doctrinal or normative was found. The claim that the field is mostly normative is supported indirectly (Batool et al.; Strauss et al.) rather than by a direct count.
- No systematic review of AI incident-reporting regimes' effectiveness was found.

## Q3. Status and scholarly treatment of frontier AI laws (SB 53, NY RAISE) and AI incident reporting

### Takeaway
California SB 53, the Transparency in Frontier AI Act, was signed on 29 Sept 2025 and has applied since 1 Jan 2026. New York's RAISE Act was signed in December 2025 and amended on 27 March 2026 to align with SB 53; it takes effect on 1 Jan 2027. Both are transparency and incident-reporting regimes, not substantive safety mandates. Scholarly treatment so far is mostly law-firm alerts, think-tank comparisons and short law-school commentary, and no empirical evaluation exists yet. Incident data infrastructure (AIID, OECD AIM) is large and growing but news-based.

### Cited Findings
**SB 53 (California)**
- Signed 29 Sept 2025; requirements in force from 1 Jan 2026. It applies to frontier models trained with more than 10^26 FLOP. "Large frontier developers" (over $500M revenue) must publish frontier AI frameworks and transparency reports summarising catastrophic-risk assessments. Its focus shifted to transparency and post-deployment reporting, away from the strict liability and pre-launch testing of the vetoed SB 1047 — [Stanford Law CodeX gap analysis, Jan 2026](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/01/11/californias-transparency-in-frontier-artificial-intelligence-act-gap-analysis/); [Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/print/v2/content/43018/california-lawmakers-pass-landmark-ai-transparency-law-for-frontier-models%3A-how-sb-53-differs-from-last-year's-failed-attempt.pdf); [National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/californias-sb-53-understanding-obligations-effect-now-and-2027)
- Critical safety incidents must be reported to Cal OES within 15 days of discovery, or within 24 hours to appropriate authorities where there is imminent risk of death or serious injury. Members of the public may also report to OES. Enforcement is by the Attorney General. The maximum civil penalty was cut from $10M to $1M during passage; secondary sources describe it as up to $1M per violation, which should be checked against the statute — [Assembly analysis](https://apcp.assembly.ca.gov/media/1019); [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/ja/insights/2025/10/california-enacts-sb-53-setting-new-standards-for-frontier-ai-safety-disclosures); [Wharton Accountable AI Lab](https://ai-analytics.wharton.upenn.edu/wharton-accountable-ai-lab/sb-53-what-californias-new-ai-safety-law-means-for-developers/)
- Scholarly critique (snippet-verified): a Stanford gap analysis concludes that SB 53 delivers "transparency and incident reporting without substantive safety mandates". A Stanford essay ("California's Disclosure Gambit", Jan 2026) argues it would create an appearance of oversight without preventing a Chernobyl-type event. Federal preemption is the main open legal question — [Stanford gap analysis](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/01/11/californias-transparency-in-frontier-artificial-intelligence-act-gap-analysis/); [Stanford "Disclosure Gambit"](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/01/15/californias-disclosure-gambit-what-sb-53-reveals-about-our-relationship-with-potentially-dangerous-technology-2/); [Built In](https://builtin.com/articles/california-sb53-law-analysis)

**NY RAISE Act**
- Signed by Gov. Hochul in December 2025 with an agreed chapter amendment. The chapter amendment was signed on 27 March 2026, and the law takes effect on 1 Jan 2027. It aligns with SB 53: a $500M revenue threshold for large frontier developers, transparency reports before deploying new or substantially modified frontier models, a public list of filers maintained by the regulator, and a fee on large developers. Civil penalties are up to $1M for a first violation and $3M for subsequent ones — [Cooley, Mar 2026](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-03-31-new-yorks-frontier-ai-law-gets-a-california-makeover-with-some-key-differences); [Wiley](https://www.wiley.law/alert-New-York-Finalizes-RAISE-Act-for-Frontier-AI-Models-Law-Takes-Effect-January-1-2027); [DWT, Dec 2025](https://www.dwt.com/blogs/artificial-intelligence-law-advisor/2025/12/new-york-raise-act-ai-safety-rules-developers)
- Comparative analysis: FPF, "The RAISE Act vs. SB 53: A Tale of Two Frontier AI Laws" — [FPF](https://fpf.org/blog/the-raise-act-vs-sb-53-a-tale-of-two-frontier-ai-laws/)
- Uncertain: one search summary says the original RAISE text required annual third-party audits, and it is unclear whether the amendment kept them. This was not confirmed and should be checked against the enrolled chapter amendment before it is stated.

**Incident reporting and databases**
- The AI Incident Database (Responsible AI Collaborative) publishes periodic roundups. Feb–Apr 2026 added 109 new IDs (Incidents 1362–1470). Aug–Oct 2026 added 179 new IDs (1619–1797). So IDs reached about 1,800 by late Oct 2026. IDs are not the same as unique incidents, and many predate their addition — [AIID Feb–Apr 2026](https://incidentdatabase.ai/blog/incident-report-2026-february-march-april/); [AIID Aug–Oct 2026](https://incidentdatabase.ai/blog/incident-report-2026-august-september-october/)
- AI Index 2025, citing AIID, reports 233 AI incidents in 2024, a record and up 56.4% from 2023 — [Our World in Data/AI Index](https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/annual-reported-ai-incidents-controversies?tab=chart)
- The OECD AI Incidents and Hazards Monitor (AIM) is an automated, news-based "media discourse monitor" (beta). It classifies events as incident, hazard or complementary information using AI-generated labels. Counter snapshots on cached pages read about 14,557 (late April 2026) and about 17,930 (late Sept 2026); these are unofficial snapshots. OECD notes that incidents' share of all AI news has fallen even as raw counts rise — [OECD.AI incidents](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/17750)

### Inferences
- Because SB 53 has applied since 1 Jan 2026, published frontier frameworks and transparency reports now exist as a public corpus. That makes compliance and quality content analysis of SB 53 disclosures a realistic, novel 2026 study, and none appears in the scholarly literature yet.
- RAISE takes effect on 1 Jan 2027, so ex-ante comparative or doctrinal analysis is feasible but empirical analysis is not yet.
- Using news-based incident databases (AIID, AIM) as a stand-in for real harm runs into reporting bias. This limitation must be stated in any student paper and is itself a known gap.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed law-review article on SB 53 or RAISE with empirical content was found. Existing treatment is commentary.
- No public Cal OES statistics on critical safety incident reports received in 2026 were found.
- The statutory text of SB 53 and the RAISE chapter amendment was not read directly. Penalty structure and the audit question need checking against primary text.

## Q4. Public datasets for an original empirical student paper, and which questions are answerable

### Takeaway
Several free, structured datasets support single-author empirical papers: CSET AGORA (more than 1,000 laws and policies, full text and tags), AIID (about 1,800 incident IDs), OECD AIM (about 18k news-derived entries), the Charlotin AI Hallucination Cases database (about 1,600 cases by June 2026), the Gutierrez/Marchant soft-law database (634 programmes), the Strauss et al. data on IEEE DataPort, and the public SB 53 disclosures. The most feasible designs are descriptive coding studies, compliance audits of public disclosures in the LL144 "Null Compliance" style, and linking governance data to incident data, which MIT says it plans but has not finished.

### Cited Findings
- CSET/ETO AGORA holds more than 1,000 AI-focused laws and policies (US and global), with summaries, full text and thematic tags. A CSET analysis counts 147 documents from laws enacted by Congress between January 2020 and March 2025 — [ETO AGORA](https://agora.eto.tech/collections); [ETO roundup #5](https://eto.tech/blog/agora-ai-governance-tracker-roundup-5/); [ETO congressional laws blog](https://eto.tech/blog/ai-laws-enacted-by-congress/)
- The MIT AI Governance Map is public, with dashboards at airisk.mit.edu/ai-governance (risk-subdomain and actor views). It is built on AGORA — [MIT risk-subdomain view](https://airisk.mit.edu/governance/risk-subdomain)
- AIID provides incident records, taxonomies and periodic roundups — [AIID discover](https://incidentdatabase.ai/discover)
- OECD AIM provides news-based incident and hazard entries with AI-generated classifications — [OECD.AI incident example](https://oecd.ai/en/incidents/17750)
- The Damien Charlotin AI Hallucination Cases database grew from about 200 cases (mid-2025) to 719 (Jan 2026), 1,227 (early Apr 2026), 1,598 (9 Jun 2026) and 1,624 (18 Jun 2026). It adds just under 8 per day. US cases numbered 1,058 by early June 2026 (about 720 federal and 340 state), and about half involve pro se litigants. The counts come from secondary sources; cite the live database with an access date — [UNC Law Library, June 2026](https://library.law.unc.edu/2026/06/handling-hallucinations-a-reflection/); [haqq.ai tracker analysis](https://haqq.ai/blog/ai-legal-hallucination-audit.html)
- The Strauss et al. replication data is on IEEE DataPort — [IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/real-world-gaps-ai-governance-research-data)
- An AI safety/ethics paper corpus of 3,550 papers, including FAccT and AIES, has open code and data at github.com/gyevnarb/ai-safety-ethics — [arXiv 2603.14495](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.14495v1)
- The ASU soft-law database holds 634 programmes (2001–2019) — [OECD.AI Wonk](https://oecd.ai/en/wonk/soft-law-2-0)
- Sandbox inventory: Datasphere counts 66 sandboxes, 31 of them AI-specific, across 44 countries — [Datasphere](https://www.thedatasphere.org/datasphere-publish/sandboxes-for-ai/)
- Methodological precedent for low-cost compliance audits: Null Compliance coded 391 employers' public web disclosures — [arXiv 2406.01399](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.01399v1)

**Questions realistically answerable in one student paper with public data**
1. **SB 53 disclosure compliance and quality audit.** Do the developers in scope publish frontier AI frameworks and transparency reports, and how do these score against the statutory elements? The design mirrors Null Compliance. Data: company websites, the Assembly analysis, and the statute. It is feasible because disclosures have been required since 1 Jan 2026. The scope set is small, so this is a qualitative or rubric study.
2. **Hallucination-sanctions analysis.** Using the Charlotin database, how do sanctions vary by court level, jurisdiction, lawyer versus pro se litigant, and time? Does the adoption of standing orders or court AI rules correlate with fewer incidents? This is a large-N public dataset and is highly feasible.
3. **Governance coverage versus incident distribution.** Cross-tabulate AGORA/MIT sector and risk coverage against AIID or AIM incident sectors to test MIT's claim of consumer-sector gaps. This is the linkage MIT says it plans. Feasible, but it needs a careful crosswalk between taxonomies, and news-based incidents carry reporting bias.
4. **From soft law to hard law.** Trace which elements of voluntary frontier-safety commitments (lab frameworks, Seoul commitments) were codified in SB 53 and RAISE. This is a doctrinal and comparative design, feasible with public texts.
5. **Replicating Strauss et al. on legal scholarship.** Classify a sample of AI-law papers (for example from SSRN or law-journal databases) as pre-deployment or deployment, and empirical or normative. Feasible at small N, but SSRN access is limited.
6. **EU AI Act literature share.** Update the 2502.03468 WoS bibliometric (October 2024 snapshot) to 2026. This is feasible only with institutional WoS or Scopus access, so availability needs checking.

### Inferences
- Questions 1 and 2 give the best mix of novelty, public data and scope. Question 1 directly addresses the "effectiveness, compliance and post-deployment" gap that reviews converge on.
- Questions about effectiveness (whether a law reduced harm) are not realistically answerable in one paper, because no counterfactual exists, laws are recent, and incident data is news-based. Student papers should frame claims as compliance, coverage or descriptive measures, not effectiveness.

### Gaps
- IAPP trackers (US state AI legislation tracker, global AI law tracker) and EU AI Act or GDPR-on-AI enforcement trackers were not verified in this session. Their access terms, since some IAPP resources are members-only, and their exact contents need checking.
- No public dataset of AI-specific enforcement actions (for example FTC "Operation AI Comply", or EU DPA actions against AI firms) was verified.
- No live counts for AIID unique incidents or OECD AIM totals were obtained from the sites themselves. Figures above are ID ranges and cached counter snapshots.
- Live access to arxiv.org, ssrc.org, airisk.mit.edu and law.stanford.edu would be needed to verify the snippet-derived figures. Those hosts have to be allowed in the session's network settings.
