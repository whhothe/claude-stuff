## Q1. What are the key cases and their verified status as of late 2026?

### Takeaway
The generative-AI chatbot product-liability field rests on one substantive federal ruling: *Garcia v. Character Technologies* (M.D. Fla., May 21, 2025), which declined to treat LLM output as speech at the pleading stage and let product-liability claims go forward. Garcia then settled in January 2026, before any appellate review. The ChatGPT cases, led by *Raine v. OpenAI*, were coordinated in San Francisco Superior Court as JCCP No. 5431, "In re ChatGPT Product Liability Cases," in February 2026. As far as I can find, no merits ruling on product status, Section 230 or the First Amendment had issued there by November 2026. Character Technologies filed for Chapter 11 in October 2026, which complicates the remaining Character.AI litigation.

### Cited Findings

**Garcia v. Character Technologies (M.D. Fla., No. 6:24-cv-01903, Judge Anne C. Conway)**
- Filed in late 2024 over the death of 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III. It is described as the first wrongful-death suit against an AI chatbot company. Sources disagree on whether it was filed in October or November 2024. — [Tech Justice Law Project case page](https://techjusticelaw.org/cases/garcia-v-character-technologies-google-and-character-ai-co-founders-daniel-de-frietas-and-noam-shazeer/); [Humane Tech case study](https://www.humanetech.com/case-study/litigation-case-study-character-ai-and-google)
- May 21, 2025 order on motions to dismiss:
  - Judge Conway said the defendants had not explained why words strung together by an LLM are speech. She was "not prepared" to hold that the chatbot's output is speech "at this stage."
  - She did hold that Character Technologies can assert its users' First Amendment right to *receive* the chatbot's "speech."
  - Claims against Google, for its alleged role in developing Character.AI, were allowed to proceed. — [Courthouse News](https://courthousenews.com/florida-judge-rules-ai-chatbots-not-protected-by-first-amendment/); [WLRN/AP](https://www.wlrn.org/law-justice/2025-05-22/orlando-teens-suicide-judge-ai-chatbots); [MTSU First Amendment Encyclopedia (AP reprint)](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/post/in-lawsuit-over-teens-death-judge-rejects-arguments-that-ai-chatbots-have-free-speech-rights/)
- On product status, commentators report that the court treated the Character.AI app as a "product" rather than a service for product-liability purposes, as far as the claims target design defects. — [Transparency Coalition](https://www.transparencycoalition.ai/news/important-early-ruling-in-characterai-case-this-chatbot-is-a-product-not-speech) **[secondary]**; [Goldberg Segalla note](https://www.goldbergsegalla.com/news-and-knowledge/news/peter-gregory-authors-article-on-ramifications-of-major-federal-ai-ruling/) **[secondary]**

  - My recollection is also that some claims, such as intentional infliction of emotional distress (IIED), were dismissed. **[UNVERIFIED]**
- Settlement:
  - Character.AI and Google agreed to an undisclosed settlement in January 2026.
  - Judge Conway entered a settlement order on Jan. 7, 2026 and gave the parties 90 days to finalize.
  - The terms reportedly include new safety features for users under 18.
  - One source says the deal covered five lawsuits.

### Inferences
- No appellate court has ruled on whether an LLM chatbot is a "product," whether Section 230 covers generated output, or whether LLM output is protected speech. *Garcia* settled, *Walters* turned on defamation elements, and the JCCP has not reached demurrers on these issues. This is the central open doctrinal space.
- Because of the bankruptcy, any Character.AI plaintiffs not covered by the January 2026 settlement face the automatic stay and a likely claims process. That shifts the questions from tort doctrine toward bankruptcy and AG enforcement.
- Litigation is moving from private tort to public enforcement: the Kentucky and Florida AG suits and the multistate bankruptcy intervention.

## Q2. What has 2023–2026 scholarship argued on products, Section 230 and First Amendment protection for AI output?

### Takeaway
The core theory literature is dense and was largely laid down in 2023–2024: the *Journal of Free Speech Law* 2023 AI symposium, Salib, Sharkey and Choi. Opinion splits on whether AI output is speech: Volokh, Lemley and Henderson say users' and developers' rights likely protect it; Salib says it is no one's speech. There is near-consensus that Section 230 likely does *not* protect output a model generates itself (Perault, the Section 230 authors), though Techdirt and Chamber of Progress dissent. A growing torts literature favors products liability or a negligence/professional-standard approach. Scholarship applying these theories to the *actual* 2025–26 dockets appears thin.

### Cited Findings
**First Amendment and speech**
- Eugene Volokh, Mark Lemley & Peter Henderson, "Freedom of Speech and AI Output," *Journal of Free Speech Law* (2023 AI & speech symposium). They argue AI output may be protected through the rights of creators to speak and of users to speak and to listen. — [JFSL post](https://journalspeech.substack.com/p/journal-of-free-speech-law-wheres-62e); [Reason/Volokh](https://reason.com/?p=8245506)
- Peter N. Salib, "AI Outputs Are Not Protected Speech," *Washington University Law Review* (2024; the search summary gives vol. 83 and the PDF begins at p. 83). He argues that when a generative system outputs text, "no one thereby expresses themselves," so the output is no one's protected speech. Garcia's "not prepared to hold that output is speech" is cited as consistent with this. — [SSRN 4687558](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4687558); [WUSTL L. Rev. PDF](https://wustllawreview.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/11_Salib_FINAL-10.2.24-1.pdf); [Lawfare follow-on, "If AI Outputs Aren't Speech, Who Has to Prove They're Human?"](https://www.lawfaremedia.org/article/if-ai-outputs-aren-t-speech--who-has-to-prove-they-re-human)
- Peter Henderson, Tatsunori Hashimoto & Mark Lemley, "Where's the Liability in Harmful AI Speech?," 3 *J. Free Speech L.* (2023). The page cite needs checking: Stanford lists both 589 and 489–588. They examine defamation, speech integral to criminal conduct and wrongful death. They conclude AI should not be categorically immune, warn against overbroad liability, and urge attention to technical design. — [Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/wheres-the-liability-in-harmful-ai-speech/); [Stanford HAI summary](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/who-liable-when-generative-ai-says-something-harmful)
- Lyrissa Lidsky & Andrew Daves (author names inferred from the PDF filename), "Inevitable Errors: Defamation by Hallucination in AI Reasoning Models," *J. Free Speech L.* — [JFSL PDF](https://www.journaloffreespeechlaw.org/lidskydaves.pdf) (title and journal confirmed by search; year and authors not confirmed)
- John G. Browning, "Whose Bot Is It Anyway? Determining Liability for AI-Generated Content," 45 *N. Ill. U. L. Rev.* (2025). It covers whether chatbot output is "speech" for First Amendment and Section 230 purposes. — [NIU Huskie Commons](https://huskiecommons.lib.niu.edu/niulr/vol45/iss3/1/)

**Section 230**

### Inferences
- The theory is saturated: product versus service, speech versus non-speech, and §230's "information content provider" analysis have all been argued thoroughly. A student paper that only restates these positions would add little.
- What is under-theorized is how the doctrines interact in actual pleadings. Examples: Garcia's design-defect versus "ideas and expressions" split; OpenAI's "software based service" framing in the JCCP; listener-rights standing as a route to First Amendment defenses; and how settlements and bankruptcy cut off precedent.

## Q3. What explicit research gaps or calls for future research do scholars identify?

### Takeaway
The explicit calls I could verify are general:
- Henderson et al. call for attention to technical design rather than categorical rules.
- Abraham & Sharkey call for matching liability regimes to particular harms.
- Grimm et al. call for uniform local rules over individual standing orders.
- Dahl et al. offer a hallucination typology as a framework for future research.
- "Who Checks the Citations?" (2026) says sanctions are reactive and uneven, and that pro se litigants are most exposed.

The biggest gap is empirical, and nobody has filled it systematically: how courts actually rule, and how sanctions vary.

### Cited Findings
- Henderson, Hashimoto & Lemley warn against broad liability and urge attention to how foundation models are technically built when assigning liability. — [Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/wheres-the-liability-in-harmful-ai-speech/)
- Abraham & Sharkey argue that different AI applications and harms should be governed "alternately by negligence, products liability, strict liability, and/or legislatively-created approaches." This implies case-by-case mapping is still to be done. — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6293099)
- Dahl et al. present their typology of legal hallucinations as "a conceptual framework for future research." — [Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/large-legal-fictions-profiling-legal-hallucinations-in-large-language-models)
- "Who Checks the Citations? Benchmarking Legal Hallucination Detection" (arXiv 2606.21155, 2026). It says sanctions are reactive and unevenly applied and that pro se litigants are disproportionately affected. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21155) (not fetched; the summary describes Henderson-lab authorship, which is **[unverified]**)
- A California Senate Judiciary analysis of a 2026 companion-chatbot bill (AB 2023, Wicks) flags unresolved First Amendment questions for chatbot laws, which may both restrict and compel speech. It notes that other design laws have been enjoined, but that the Ninth Circuit rejected one facial challenge. — [Cal. Senate Judiciary analysis](https://sjud.senate.ca.gov/system/files/2026-06/ab-2023-wicks-sjud-analysis.pdf)

### Inferences
Candidate open questions for a student paper:
1. How do courts actually draw the line between "design" and "content/expression" in chatbot product claims? The Garcia distinction has not been tested elsewhere.
2. Does the "listener's right to receive" theory accepted in Garcia let developers raise First Amendment defenses even if output is not their speech? This bridges Salib and Volokh.
3. Do companion-chatbot statutes (SB 243 / SB 1119) survive First Amendment scrutiny after *NetChoice*-style challenges?
4. How do settlement and bankruptcy suppress precedent in AI tort law?

## Q4. How much scholarship exists on AI hallucinated citations and court AI rules, and what is still unstudied?

### Takeaway
Two things are well documented: measuring how often models hallucinate (Dahl et al., 2024; the Stanford study of legal research tools) and court incidents (Charlotin's database, about 2,200 decisions worldwide by late October 2026). Systematic empirical *legal* scholarship on sanctions outcomes and on how well court AI rules work appears sparse. I found commentary in Judicature and the trade press, and trackers, but no law-review empirical study of sanctions or standing orders. This is the most tractable gap for a student.

### Cited Findings
- **Dahl, Magesh, Suzgun & Ho**, "Large Legal Fictions: Profiling Legal Hallucinations in Large Language Models," *Journal of Legal Analysis* 16(1): 64–93 (2024), doi:10.1093/jla/laae003.
  - Hallucination rates on verifiable federal-case questions ranged from 58% (GPT-4) to 88% (Llama 2).
  - Models often fail to correct a user's false premises and cannot always tell when they are hallucinating.
  - Risks are greatest for pro se litigants. — [Stanford Law](https://law.stanford.edu/publications/large-legal-fictions-profiling-legal-hallucinations-in-large-language-models); [JLA PDF](https://dho.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/Hallucinations%5FJLA.pdf)
- A Stanford study of commercial legal research AI tools found hallucination rates of about 17–33%; vendors disputed the method. (This is Magesh et al., "Hallucination-Free?", arXiv 2405.20362, later published in the *Journal of Empirical Legal Studies* per my recollection **[venue UNVERIFIED]**.) — reported in [search summary of trackers](https://www.aiforlawyersacademy.com/blog/ai-hallucinations-court-sanctions/) **[secondary]**

## Q5. What is the state of AI companion chatbots and minors, including California SB 243?

### Takeaway
SB 243 (Padilla) is verified:
- signed Oct. 13, 2025, effective Jan. 1, 2026;
- requires disclosure, suicide and self-harm protocols, and minor-specific safeguards;
- gives a private right of action for "injury in fact," with at least $1,000 per violation (sources differ on the exact formula);
- requires annual reporting from July 1, 2027.

Newsom vetoed the stricter AB 1064 in 2025. He then signed SB 1119 ("Adam's Law," Padilla/Wicks/Bauer-Kahan) on Sept. 10, 2026, with most requirements taking effect July 1, 2027. Scholarship specific to companion-chatbot laws is thin; most writing is law-firm client alerts.

## Q6. Is this a feasible and strong topic for an original student paper using public sources?

### Takeaway
Hotness: (a) chatbot product liability is **very hot** in scholarship but **theory-saturated**. (b) Companion chatbots and minors is **hot in policy, thin in scholarship**. (c) AI in courts is **very hot in practice and press, thin in empirical legal scholarship**.

The strongest original-contribution options:
1. An empirical coding study of the Charlotin database plus underlying opinions: what predicts sanction severity, and do standing orders matter.
2. A doctrinal paper on the "design versus expression" line after *Garcia*, using JCCP 5431 filings.
3. A First Amendment analysis of SB 243 / SB 1119.

Feasibility is high for (1). It is moderate for (2), because California Superior Court JCCP filings are less accessible than federal PACER/RECAP filings.

### Cited Findings
- Charlotin's database is public and has about 2,200 decisions with party-type and country breakdowns. — [haqq.ai summary](https://www.haqq.ai/blog/ai-legal-hallucination-audit); [Charlotin media page](https://www.damiencharlotin.com/media/)
- *Garcia* is a federal case (M.D. Fla. 6:24-cv-01903), so its filings are reachable via PACER/RECAP and CourtListener. Press summaries quote the May 2025 order. — [Courthouse News](https://courthousenews.com/florida-judge-rules-ai-chatbots-not-protected-by-first-amendment/)
- *Raine* and the JCCP are on the San Francisco Superior Court docket, also indexed by Trellis (partly paywalled). — [Trellis](https://trellis.law/case/cgc25628528/matthew-raine-et-al-vs-openai-inc-a-delaware-corporation-et-al)
- Statutes and legislative analyses are free: SB 243 chaptered text, Senate Judiciary analyses, and FRE 707 committee materials (via uscourts.gov). — [CalMatters Digital Democracy](https://calmatters.digitaldemocracy.org/bills/ca_202520260sb243); [Cal. Senate Judiciary](https://sjud.senate.ca.gov/system/files/2026-06/ab-2023-wicks-sjud-analysis.pdf)
- Key scholarship is open access: JFSL is free online; Salib is on SSRN and the WUSTL L. Rev. site; Sharkey is on the Sedona Conference site; Dahl et al. is in JLA, open access. — links in Q2 and Q4.

### Inferences
- **(a) Product liability**
  - Hotness: very high.
  - Established: the main positions on product status, speech and §230.
  - Unresolved: any appellate holding.
  - Originality risk: high, since many articles exist and the litigation is live and may change.
  - Best original angle: map the arguments actually pleaded in JCCP 5431 and the Garcia briefs against the scholarly positions, or analyze how settlement and bankruptcy prevent precedent.
- **(b) Companion chatbots and minors**
  - Hotness: high in policy, low in scholarship.
  - Originality opportunity: good, with a First Amendment analysis of SB 243 / SB 1119 / NY law in light of recent NetChoice litigation.
  - Risk: the statutes are changing fast.
- **(c) AI in courts**
  - Hotness: very high in press, moderate in scholarship.
  - Originality opportunity: **strongest.** A systematic coded dataset (for example, a sample of 200–300 U.S. decisions from the Charlotin database, coded for sanction type and amount, filer type, court, presence of a standing order and admission of AI use) would establish something new with fully public sources.
  - A second angle: judges' own AI errors (Wingate/Neals) and court-internal AI policies.
- Overall recommendation: (c) gives the best ratio of novelty to feasibility. (b) is second. (a) is viable only with a narrow empirical or docket-based framing.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm whether someone has already published an empirical sanctions study in 2026. A student should search SSRN for "hallucination sanctions empirical" and Charlotin's own publications before committing.
- Whether JCCP 5431 filings are free online, or only through the court's paid portal or Trellis, was not verified.
